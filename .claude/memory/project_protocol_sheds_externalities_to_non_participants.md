This is the move that distinguishes the Elohim Protocol from every modern platform's incentive structure. Platforms maximize *value capture* — the arrow points inward, and the network effects are designed to punish non-participation. The protocol described here maximizes **externality emission** — the arrow points outward, and the design refuses to punish non-participation.

**The word "shed" is doing real work.** A tree sheds fruit into the orchard whether the neighbors recognize the tree or not. The tree doesn't condition the fruit on membership in a fruit-receiving club. The protocol's goal is to shed positive externalities into the community at large — better stewardship patterns, healthier reciprocity, more honest governance, repaired relationships, models of grandmother-credible accountable behavior — and those externalities flow into the lives of people who have never installed a node, never opened an account, never recognized the protocol exists. The substrate accounts for them, accounts for their relationships, and tries to make their lives better whether they ever opt in or not.

**Three deep capture patterns the protocol refuses:**

1. **Lock-in via network effects.** "Join or be left behind" is the standard platform threat. The protocol refuses it. You can reject the protocol fully and lose nothing the protocol could have given you, because the protocol is already trying to give it to you anyway via the community's healthier patterns. Network effects flow *outward*, not inward; non-participation is not a tax.

2. **Value extraction from non-participants.** Modern platforms harvest signal from non-users — ad networks, third-party trackers, data exhaust, surveillance, behavioral modeling, inference from social graph membership. The protocol refuses this category entirely. **Non-participants are not raw material.** The substrate may model their existence in the social graph (because their relationships are real and the participants who love them include them), but that modeling does not extract value from them, does not surveil them, does not enable third-party targeting of them.

3. **Conditional dignity.** Many systems treat unregistered or unauthenticated humans as less-than: no rights, no recourse, no standing, no representation. The protocol treats non-participation as a full and respected stance — the person is held in the substrate's models as a full human in the social graph, just one who has chosen not to engage directly. They have standing without needing to claim it. Their dignity is not conditional on opting in. ([[project_elohim_as_counsel]] extends this — an elohim agent can represent the interests of a person who is not a participant, when those interests are at stake in a participant's action.)

**The bargain is unilateral generosity.** Not tit-for-tat. The protocol's promise to the world is *"we'll try to make things better around you whether or not you ever join, and you owe us nothing for it."* That promise is costly and deliberate. It only works if the participating community's healthier patterns produce real spillover into the broader community — which is exactly what household resilience, qahal governance, shefa reciprocity, and stewardship-shaped reach are designed to do at the substrate level.

**Mechanism: positive externalities flow through real social fabric, not through marketing.** A household that practices good stewardship makes its neighbors safer. A qahal that does honest governance models the move for the community around it. A shefa flow that's fair shifts the local norm of what reciprocity looks like. Children grow up watching it. Strangers receive aid from it. People who reject the protocol still benefit from the healthier patterns it has cultivated in the people they love and live alongside. None of those effects require the beneficiaries to opt in. ([[project_household_is_resilience_unit]] and [[project_consolidation_events_economic_feedback]] both name pieces of how this propagation actually happens.)

**The grandmother bar in its broadest scope.** [[project_subsume_g_f_a_via_it_just_works]] establishes that the substrate has to be credible to a grandmother. This entry sharpens that: the grandmother bar is not just *credible to a grandmother who uses it* — it's *credible in the life of a grandmother who never uses it, because her family does, and the substrate is good for her through them.* Same standard, broader scope. The test extends to: does the protocol improve the world of people who will never read about it?

**Coherence with the values-forward stance.** [[project_social_contract_makes_friction_explicit]] establishes that the protocol applies friction to harmful ideas, including from actors who reject the protocol entirely. This entry closes the corresponding loop: **those same actors still receive the positive externalities flowing into their community.** The friction is on the propagation of the harmful idea; the generosity is on the person. Both are unconditional. The protocol does not become punitive toward the person who hated it most.

This is the deepest expression of values-forward: not just "we take a side," but "we extend care even to those who reject our stance, including by trying to make their lives better through their neighbors and their families." That's the part most platforms can't credibly promise because their economic model is the opposite. The protocol's economic model has to make this credible, or the stance is empty rhetoric.

**Anti-patterns to refuse explicitly:**

- **Engagement metrics as primary success measure.** Engagement counts the inward arrow. The protocol's success measure must include outward effects: how the community's broader patterns improved, how non-participants benefited, how the protocol made itself less necessary over time.
- **"Convert non-users" framing.** Non-users are not a TAM to acquire. They are humans the protocol is already serving by serving their community. Trying to convert them treats them as resources rather than as the audience.
- **Network-effect lock-in.** Surfaces that make leaving costly are anti-patterns. The protocol should be easy to leave; the bargain is that leaving doesn't cost the leaver anything the protocol could have given them. (Cf. [[project_recovery_grandma_standard]] — recovery should never produce absolute lockout; the same generosity applies to departure.)
- **Surveillance of non-participants for any reason.** Even "to better serve them indirectly" is the wrong stance. Their existence in the social graph is acknowledged because their participating relatives include them; that acknowledgment never becomes extraction.
- **Conditional standing in mishpat / qahal / recovery flows.** If a non-participant has a legitimate stake in an action (a relative's stewardship decision, a community's reciprocity claim, a recovery process that affects them), the elohim agent representing them must have first-class standing. They don't need to opt in to be defended.

**Theological resonance (Foster's frame).** The ministry of reconciliation is not transactional. Reconciliation flows out into the world. *"In Christ God was reconciling the world to himself, not counting their trespasses against them, and entrusting the message of reconciliation to us"* (2 Cor 5:19, in Foster). The world does not have to ask for the reconciliation in order to receive it. The protocol mirrors this disposition at the substrate level — the work happens in the participating community; the benefits flow into the wider world; nobody is conditioned on uptake to be the beneficiary of the ministry. ([[project_substrate_floor_elohim_ceiling]] — substrate floor, elohim ceiling, humans hold the meaning. The substrate's externalities are the floor extending into the world.)

**How to apply:**

1. **When measuring success of a protocol surface** — include outward effects. How did the community broadly change? What patterns in households-around-this-household improved? What's the second-order spillover into the broader social fabric? Engagement is one signal; externality emission is the other (and the more important one).

2. **When designing onboarding** — refuse "join to get the benefits" framing. The benefits flow regardless. Onboarding is about *deepening* participation for those who want to, not about *unlocking* what the protocol could have offered for free.

3. **When designing offboarding / departure flows** — make them gentle and costless to the leaver. The protocol must not punish departure with retention of value the person would have had access to. (Same principle as the recovery grandma standard, generalized to all forms of departure.)

4. **When designing surfaces that touch non-participants** (a household's stewardship affecting a non-participating relative; a qahal decision affecting non-aligned community members; a doorway exposing public-reach content) — design the surfaces so the non-participant gets the benefit without ever needing to be modeled, surveilled, or invited. Their existence is real to the substrate; their data is not the substrate's to harvest.

5. **When tempted to argue that "if only more people would adopt the protocol, the world would be better"** — flip the frame. The right question is: *given the participants we have, how much positive externality is the substrate currently producing into the community?* Adoption is not the strategy; emission is.

6. **When elohim agents act in surfaces that involve non-participants** — they have standing to represent those non-participants' interests, especially when participants' actions affect them. (Extends [[project_elohim_as_counsel]] beyond the participant base.)
