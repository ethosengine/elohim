Operator ruling, 2026-10-05, on device approval and provisioning: when a person's identity is held by more than one of their own nodes, approving a new device must NOT require all of them by default. One controller acts; the others affirm later. Requiring several is an option a person may choose at setup for an adversarial environment, never the default. Witnesses (an attending elohim) are fully automatic: a beat in the code where a peer could pause, inspect claims, enrich provenance and sign, which today passes straight through.

**Why:** "Trust and affirm later" is the trustful pattern of the architecture: trust builds up gradually without becoming blocking, and the project has to live by its own ceremonies in day-to-day development. I had recommended "both must sign" as hardening; the operator rejected it as the default.

**How to apply:** In any ceremony design, default every additional party (second controller, witness, doorway) to strengthening-after-the-fact. Offer a required quorum only as a declared per-identity option. The doorway confers only web2 conveniences (account recovery) and optionally facilitates a ceremony; no provisioning path may need one. Provisioning must be tested along three paths: doorway-chaperoned conductor -> device steward; device first (Che with its conductor as first steward, no doorway) -> second device forms the basic network with no doorway; device steward -> doorway steward added later. Local-first provisioning optionally follows the Agency Phase ladder. Same day, on pairing over a carrier (local discovery, or a doorway's relay): the operator called it one pattern everywhere — pairing a home device, sliding a blade into a rack, adding a node to a cluster. A new node announces itself unassigned; whoever operates the receiving node decides who its operator and stewards of record are and configures it. That operator is a person today and an elohim node operator on a mature network, with no human intervention. So the decision is a seam with interchangeable deciders (person, declaration, attending elohim), and every flow has equivalent simple CLI and UI forms over one node-local API (my interpretation: the elohim consumes that same API, not a private one). Also same day, corrected by the operator after I over-protected it: a node that provisioned itself before the real human was known joins by a recommended click-through default, not a forced decision (behavioural economics: do not always ask the operator). The pre-provisioned node always gets a confirmation beat so it knows what is about to happen to it. Deleting its identity and re-keying is fine when nothing developed under it. If history accidentally developed, that history is what needs care: reconciling it needs a p2p reconciliation surface that is unbuilt, and reconciling bespoke networks is an epic-level concern of the protocol, not a slice. The operator framed it as the protocol's usual shape: deterministic floor (forget and re-join) and elohim ceiling (formal reconcile). And three things stay distinct on a node: whose it is (the person whose identity it speaks for), who operates it, and (not a separate class) that every authorized device speaks for that person. (I first made 'speaks for' a smaller set reached by a separate act; the operator corrected that: every authorized device speaks for its person, recognized within the private reach of the devices they steward, and any of them can approve the next by default, because the network holds the root, not a device. The separate act they agreed to was about people: who operates a node is not whose it is.) Never call a node a steward: the operator corrected me the same day, the human is the steward and a node only speaks for them (the zome's word for such a key is controller). The operator's example: grandma needs a node, the grandson-in-law operates it, and where there is no suitable relative the elohim and the commons fulfil the operator role. Operating confers no say over the identity. Finally, the operator settled a question I should not have asked: this is the OAuth model and it is settled there. A username or sign-in word is an identity claim and is shown as one; a signed-in device acts with the person's authority. The only thing added is that witnesses have a beat to notice when something does not feel right (a child banging on the keyboard; a service detecting an under-age user and locking until the login is re-authenticated) and may pause for re-authentication. That adds no mental load for the human. So do not design per-act proof of the person, and do not treat 'the node cannot tell the person from whoever is at the machine' as a gap to close by asking the human more; it is the witness's job. Witnessed validation moments exist everywhere (a reach gate is another); the device work only set the default moments at the sign-in and authorization floor, so shape a witness beat as one moment among many, not as a feature of one ceremony. Standing preference stated the same day: keep security primitives as close to the machine as possible (keystore, session keys, sign-in), for reliability, for security, and so platform capabilities such as biometrics are reachable later. Prefer the platform's own key store and authenticator over an application-level secret, and a local channel over a network listener for same-machine acts. What keeps any-device-approves honest is the witness and affirmation of the person's other devices: it reveals who they are and what they steward, and lets an elohim look at the context or affirm the ceremony from one device to the next. So affirmation and its visibility are load-bearing in the design, not a later nicety.
