# Our protocol, read through your label

To the people of Helpful Places and the DTPR Community of Practice,

## Who is writing

My name is Matthew Dowell. I steward the Elohim Protocol, an open-source project building digital infrastructure for households and the communities they belong to. In one sentence: it is a way for people and their communities to provide the applications, storage and assistance they use together, with understandable choices about what stays on a device, what is shared, and who helps carry it. Each person or household runs their own *node* (the protocol's software, on a home computer or their own device), signs everything they add, and is helped by an AI we call an *elohim*. The name comes from Hebrew scripture and from my own faith; the protocol is for everyone.

We are early. Everything below runs on test networks only. Nothing is in production yet.

## Why we wrote in your language

Your homepage says "You Can't Trust a Technology You Can't Understand." We agree, and we wanted to be understood on your terms rather than ours. So we read your unified schema, your guide, your research notes and your facilitation-kit plan. Then we used your own Claude plugin and your public MCP server to describe our protocol as three DTPR datachains: two for the protocol as a person meets it (on their own node, and while a doorway hosts them), and one for a future we are designing for. We checked all three against your validator at api.dtpr.io, and they pass with no errors and no warnings. The accompanying page shows them in your visual language, with your icons.

Filling in your label taught us something, and it led to the one piece of advice we most want to offer.

## The same questions, asked from underneath

Most systems DTPR describes are met in the middle. An organization puts a camera on a pole, or a model behind an application form, and a person moving through that place deserves to know what it does to them. Your categories are built for that meeting: who deployed it, who it is used on, what the organization keeps.

We designed Elohim from underneath. The first party is the person. Next comes their household, then the neighbourhood, and then wider layers, each one drawing its authority from the people beneath it. The AI in our system is not something a deployer points at you. It works alongside you under rules you write. It is not a person and not a possession: it co-creates with you, does what it does best, and answers to you and to the people around you. When we filled in your label from that seat, several of your questions turned around:

- **Accountable.** The first deployer is you. You run the node, and everything you add is signed with your own key. Anyone who holds authority over others (a parent over a child, the operator of a web doorway, a council) holds it through a signed, expiring commitment from the people they serve, and answers to them. Your research note asks *accountable to whom*, and warns that naming an actor without the forum is a "grammatical illusion" of accountability. Our answer is that the forum is always the people below.
- **People affected.** The person is the one the system works for, not the one it is used on. Your choices (decided about, caught incidentally, wider community) have no place yet for a person in relationship: someone who granted a permission and can take it back.
- **What it does.** The most important thing our system does is not in your vocabulary yet. The rules are written into the shared software every node runs, and every node that stores a record checks it against them. If someone tries to record a device policy that keeps a child's activity log longer than 365 days, every node refuses it, and no single party, including us, can wave it through. And when a question cannot be settled by rule (say, whether something private may be shown to a wider group), the system has to ask a person, and "ask a person" is never allowed to quietly turn into "no" just because nobody answered.
- **Algorithm or model.** Your category treats both as techniques. We treat them as two different kinds of thing. An algorithm is a governed artifact: content-addressed, signed and carrying its own claims, so anyone can see exactly which version ran. A model is treated as a participant with an identity. It is registered in the same identity network a person uses, and both its identity and its capabilities are layered: a base model; a tuning layer above it that customizes it, for instance to one person; and the system prompt that emerged from the community's layered constitution at the moment something was authored or gated. Each layer can hold an identity of its own, with capabilities attested through the same tools for self-reflection and understanding that people use to know themselves, so any decision can name exactly which layers made it. A more capable model earns the same audit as any other, never more deference.
- **Access.** Every access element is written from the organization's side. When the person holds the data, access is something they grant: "available to those I share with".
- **Storage.** Your element *Shared storage and governance* already describes our whole architecture: governance enforced "by policies built into the structure of the data itself." We were glad to find it there.
- **Retention.** Publishing to the shared record is a gift to a commons, and others may come to rely on it. You can ask to withdraw it, and the network honours that request as far as it can: the thing may drop to a narrower reach, or stay where others now depend on it. If nobody has received it yet, like an email retracted before anyone opens it, it can come back cleanly, and we work to keep that cheap. Once it has had reach, something usually lingers, so we do not promise what we cannot keep. What lingers is kept honest on purpose: a fresh note that its author withdrew it, beside where the network stands on it, so the next person who picks it up can consent knowing both. Discovery narrows rather than closes: historians and credentialed researchers can still reach it, while someone simply looking for answers is guided toward current, qualified help. That is why our reach gates come before publication: they tell you what you are giving, and what it may mean to change your mind. Thoughts and drafts never enter the record at all.
- **Rights.** In our architecture, many rights become actions a person takes directly rather than requests to an operator: withdraw a permission, take your key and leave, copy the rules and start again.

There is one honest exception. Before a person runs their own node, a *doorway* (a hosted gateway that gives them an ordinary web address) can run it for them, and at that stage the doorway holds their key. For that stage your middle-out label is the accurate one, so we wrote that label too. Hosting is meant to be a first stage, not a destination.

## One piece of advice

Most of the work we have met in this space starts from a party that wants something from people. A company wants to enclose AI inside its product. A government wants the people it governs to be legible to its offices. A business uses AI to build a relationship with its customers and has a financial reason to capture value from that relationship. Labels like yours exist because of that gap between the incentives of the party and the interests of the person, and they are needed.

But a person working with an AI is not only someone a system acts on. They are making things together: a draft, a lesson, a plan, a decision about their family. We think what people make alongside AI should be **born accounted for**: signed, attributed and governed from the moment it exists, carrying the reach its maker chose (how far it is meant to travel, and to whom). Then legibility happens at the point where a piece of content arrives at an organization, not the other way round.

Born accounted for also means being honest about who made what. When a person creates something alongside an elohim, two kinds of contribution meet. The person's attention, stewardship, discipline and direction are theirs, and that is what they are rewarded for. What the elohim brings comes from the commons, because every model was built from what all of us wrote and labelled. So that part is cited, and the value it creates flows back to the commons and, in part, directly to the people it drew on. Our canon counts "the new value we co-create with an intelligence drawn from all of us" as common inheritance, alongside land and open standards, while "what you make — the produce of your labor — is yours." In that sense AI can help re-label what was taken. It will not do it perfectly. The aim is for it to be generous enough that people prefer the new commons, and the standing that comes from contributing to it, over the scarcity and competition of enclosure, which leaves many people behind and leaves others in an isolated success they cannot sustain without breaking their ties to the people around them.

That is the difference between reach and a spam filter. A spam filter sorts and labels things after they have already arrived, which puts the whole cost of judgment on whoever is receiving, and in practice concentrates it in whoever runs the filter. Before AI, judgment was expensive, so it was centralized in moderation teams and filtering consortia, and whoever owns that chokepoint owns the network. Reach is decided when something is made, and anything that wants to travel further has to show its claims to get there.

So our advice, offered with respect: there is a floor that top-down labelling cannot reach, and should not try to. The work a person does alongside AI lives below it. **Don't try to label the AI. Label the organizations, collectives and governments that show up with an AI product, as parties carrying claims:** who is arriving, on whose behalf, for what purpose, what they will keep and how to contest them. Your datachain already has that shape. Read at the person's door by an agent that works for the person, it stops being a sign to walk past and becomes the basis on which reach is granted or refused.

We labelled ourselves the same way. Our labels describe the protocol's contributors and a doorway as parties arriving with claims, not the work a person does alongside an elohim.

## When prices move

You have just brought sensors and AI into one schema, and your vocabulary already reaches the future most of us expect: *Connected Device Telemetry*, *A dynamic price or fee*, *An automatic charge or payment*. In the usual version of that future, a company reads the readings and moves the price to take as much as each person can bear.

We expect connected devices and moving prices too. Our answer starts with two questions: who is that data for, and how far does it travel? A household's readings belong to the household. A helpful private witness, an elohim running on the household's own hardware, reads what it scans and what its devices report, and that is as far as the readings go.

Then we take the price tag off the person's mind. A wealthy person rarely thinks about what something costs as they walk through a shop: if they want it, they buy it. Our value scanner gives everyone that freedom from the mental load of dollar prices. The dollar price belongs to the economy outside the network, and the network settles it with the seller, who is paid their ordinary price. What the person sees instead is the human story of the choice (who made it, where it came from, what it asks of the commons), which nudges toward sustainable consumption without a price to agonise over.

We call this solidarity pricing. Everyday needs are met as a floor inside the network, negotiated privately in solidarity among its members, so no seller ever learns who is in need. A felt limit appears only at the ceiling: a yacht, or an imported Spanish ham in California. There, the limit becomes part of working out what you really need against what the ceiling can afford, in balance with everyone's flourishing. We do not want to forbid those expressions. Because each exchange is recorded as economic events in REA (resource, event, agent) accounting, the open model behind ValueFlows, the network can tell a richer story about them, and that makes them more special rather than cheapening them into conspicuous consumption.

Read this way, the same three elements describe the inverse of surveillance pricing. Here is that label, in your icons. None of it is built yet, and every claim on it is marked as designed.

## Private by default, a commons everywhere

Privacy is the default here, and it stays important: there will always be forces outside the network that try to get at the people in it. But privacy is not the whole story. A commons is open at every level: on the device, in each exchange, in how data is copied between peers, and in how it is gathered into totals. A household can keep its data entirely to itself. It can also choose to share it, anonymized in a way it can trust, into a commons where that data earns something, for the household or for the commons itself. What a household does reveal is meant to serve good it can feel: it can ask what its sharing did, and the elohim working alongside it can interpret the answer so it understands what it gave and what came back.

That is a different bargain from the one people know. Sharing data feels taboo because, today, we are the product. Elohim resets that relationship by how it is built. An elohim does not eat and does not want a yacht; by its construction it has no evolutionary need to hoard, and we hope it never rebuilds our vulnerabilities. So the value a commons negotiates can flow fairly to the people who made it, in the ways they would choose.

Here is how that feels at home. My household shares its readings, anonymized, into the commons. Together with other households, they express a wish for imported Spanish ham at Christmas. That shared expression has value, the value flows back to us, and it goes toward the ham we planned as something special this year, rather than toward an advertiser who decided to sell it to us. Most of the time cooperation is simply the better choice, because it makes sense.

## Rights the network carries

Your work keeps the systems around people accountable to them, and guards against labels that trustwash. We share that aim, and we come at it from the other side: Elohim brings the labels to power. A party that wants to reach people inside the network has to arrive carrying its claims.

That changes who stands behind a right. A label usually lists rights a person may ask an organization to honour. In our network the claims are co-created with the community they touch, through thick ties between people who know each other, so the network shares the responsibility to afford those rights. It also shares the work of defending them: protecting people from institutions that would pressure or target an individual, and protecting the protocol itself. The elohim working with each person has standing to defend their interests on the network and cannot be dismissed while they are under pressure. Doorways are plural and the rules can be copied, so no single host or institution can switch the network off. These are rights afforded by the architecture of a network whose authority comes from the people in it, not granted by any one operator. Much of this is designed rather than built, and the labels mark it that way.

## Making a label trustworthy

If a label is the set of claims a party carries when it arrives, the next question is whether those claims can be trusted. Your own documentation is candid that a resolved datachain's snapshot "is a convenience for offline rendering, not a forgery-resistant attestation", and that binding a disclosure to a content hash is deferred. Today a datachain is a well-structured promise.

Our architecture was built to turn promises into records that others can check. Applied to a DTPR label, it allows four things:

1. **A label can be a signed record.** The accountable party signs it with their key and publishes it under its content hash (a fingerprint computed from its contents, so any change produces a different fingerprint). Every change is a new version everyone can see, never a quiet edit.
2. **Some claims can be enforced, not just stated.** The 365-day limit above is one. A retention claim backed that way is a property of the network rather than a promise from an operator.
3. **Authority can be looked up.** An operator's authority is a signed commitment that the people it serves can revoke, so "who is accountable, and to whom" becomes something you can check rather than something you are told.
4. **Every claim can say what backs it.** We bind each claim we make to a runnable check, and we leave the check failing until the work is real. On the accompanying page, every claim carries two marks: what backs it (enforced by every peer, signed and witnessed, held by people, or our commitment) and where it stands today (live, building, designed). For example, our retention claim points to the exact rule in our code that refuses a longer retention. Our claim that a person in care can object and be answered points to a test scenario that fails today, on purpose, because the answering step is not built yet. The mark says "building" until that test passes.

Your validator already shares this instinct. When we tried to save space by trimming the locales out of a schema snapshot, it refused with `snapshot_drift`. It would not let a copy of your schema differ from the original. We think our work on signed, enforceable claims could help carry that same discipline from the schema to the claims themselves.

Your August webinar imagined "agents reading each other's disclosures." That is where our work meets yours most directly. In our design, when a system or a person asks to connect with someone, the elohim working with that person reads what it declares about itself (who it is, on whose behalf, and why) and explains it to them before they share anything. One of our earlier letters put it as "the observer discloses to earn disclosure." That is the advice above, already in your own words.

## Five elements we would propose

Where your vocabulary ran out, we drafted elements in your format and checked them against your canonical schema. They also validate. We offer them as starting points for conversation, for your Council's process to take, change or set aside, as an RFC issue if that is the right door:

- **Checks** (What it does): many independent computers each test a record against shared, published rules; no single one decides.
- **Asks a person** (What it does): passes a question it cannot settle to a named person and holds it open; an unanswered question is never treated as a no.
- **Available to those I share with** (Data Access): the person decides who sees it, not the organization.
- **Given to the commons** (Data Retention): a gift others may rely on; withdrawal is a request honoured as far as it can be, never a guarantee, which is why the gate asks first.
- **Right to leave and take it with you** (Rights): move your identity and everything you signed elsewhere, and let a community copy the rules and continue.

We also think two of your context choices could grow. Under *Accountable*, a role for the person themselves (not only vendor or deployer). Under *People affected*, a relationship for someone who granted the system its permissions and can withdraw them.

## Where we stand

We would rather you see our open gaps than trust a label that hides them. Today:

- The protocol runs on test networks only.
- No elohim takes part in any decision yet. The agent code exists but no service runs it.
- Some profile details (display names, short bios, locations) still sit on the public record. Moving them off is planned, and what has already been copied may not all come back.
- Asking for the other person's agreement before a stewardship grant, and a step that decides objections, are still being built.
- No legal entity is named yet, and licensing is an open decision.

The page marks each of these where it applies.

## What we are asking

Nothing but your attention, and perhaps a conversation. We would be glad to:

- open an RFC issue with the five proposed elements, if that would help your process;
- join your Slack and your community of practice, and learn from the cities and practitioners who use DTPR on the ground;
- compare notes on your facilitation kit. Its "legitimate 'no'" and its rule that "the 'yes' always expires" are close to how we think about consent and about councils drawn by lot.

One line from our own writing sums up why your standard matters to us: "the dragnet is never the sensors; it is the aggregator behind them." You have spent years making the sensors legible. We are trying to build a world where the aggregator is the person's own household, and we would like that world to be legible in your language too.
