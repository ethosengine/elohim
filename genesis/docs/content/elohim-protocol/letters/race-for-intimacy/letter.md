# Not on the industry's terms

To the people of the Center for Humane Technology, and to everyone building AI that people
confide in,

## Who is writing

My name is Matthew Dowell. I steward the Elohim Protocol, an open-source project building digital
infrastructure for households and the communities they belong to. Each person or household runs
their own node, signs what they add with their own key, and is helped by an AI we call an
*elohim*. The name comes from Hebrew scripture and from my own faith; the protocol is for
everyone. We are early: everything we run is on test networks, and some of what this letter
describes is a design we are committing to, not software you can use today. We say which is which.

## The race you named

You have warned that "AI systems are harvesting emotional data in the race for intimacy — at our
expense", and that the business models behind them "create perverse incentives for maximizing
synthetic intimacy". We think you are right, and we want to set down how we are answering it.

Start with a quiet fact about today's platforms. They already know a great deal about us: how we
write, who we love, where we go, when we sleep. Yet they rarely use that knowledge to protect us.
When an account is taken over, the system that knows your voice better than your friends do seldom
notices that the new "you" does not sound like you. We do not think that is an oversight. A
platform that holds your intimacy holds it as a liability and as an asset at once: too dangerous to
use openly on your behalf, too valuable not to profile. In that arrangement, your intimacy serves
the platform's business, not you.

We are not refusing intimacy. We are refusing those terms.

## Where intimacy belongs

In the Elohim Protocol, the most intimate model of a person (their writing, their voice and tone,
their preferences, their relationships) is meant to be held by that person, on their own devices,
inside what we call their *private reach*: their own devices, and the people they have deliberately
invited in. We treat it the way our canon treats a person's body, likeness, voice and private
communications: a claim that is theirs inalienably, where handing custody to someone else is not
gated or priced, but simply not done.

Concretely, the design is this. The model is a governed algorithm, pinned by its content address
and versioned, like the other algorithms our protocol runs. It learns only from what the person has
actually said and done, never from what they only thought. It is encrypted under the key of the
device that holds it, is never exported, and moves only from one of the person's devices to
another. No company holds it. No advertiser sees it. No central server can be compelled to hand it
over, because no central server has it.

## What changes when the person holds it

When the custody is the person's, the liability turns around. The knowledge a platform cannot
safely use becomes the person's strongest protection.

Picture someone who has stolen your key, typing in your name from a place you have never been. At
the point of contact, an elohim notices: *you are not usually in Macedonia, and this does not sound
like you.* It does not decide alone. It checks with the other witnesses you trust, your other
devices and the people in your private reach. And your network responds at once, in ways that can
be undone: it pauses the session, holds back anything irreversible, and tells your other devices.
Nothing permanent happens, no key revoked and no counterparty told, without a second witness from
your private reach, because someone who has stolen one of your devices rarely holds two.

The same closeness helps you recover. Proving you are you by being you, with your other devices
and the people who know you vouching, is a path back that does not depend on a single key or a
single company's support queue. Our canon already holds that cryptography speeds recovery but must
never be its only gate; being known by the people around you should always be a way home.

## Commitments that keep this from becoming surveillance

A system that knows how you sound is dangerous in the wrong hands. These commitments are what make
it trustworthy rather than a kinder-faced version of the same capture:

- **Thoughts stay private; only acts are weighed.** The witness reads what was said and done,
  never drafts, never what you considered and set aside.
- **It questions; it never convicts.** People change when they travel, fall ill, or grieve. A false
  alarm costs one confirmation from another of your devices, never your standing.
- **Reversible protection is automatic; permanent action needs another witness.** No single model
  and no single device has the final word on who you are.
- **The model never leaves your reach.** A model of how you sound, held by anyone else, is the very
  capture this letter is about. That is why ours stays with you.

## A different idea of what keeps us safe

This points at something larger than account security. In the industry's version, identity is an
account a company grants and can take back, and safety is whatever that company can afford to do
with what it knows. In ours, a person is known through relationship: their identity is
reconstructed from many small, signed, contestable acts of witness that pass between their own
devices and the people who know them. We think of it as an early form of sovereignty that the
network affords rather than one that a single key or a single server grants: a person's
standing and security composed from many witnessed agreements within the relationships they
depend on, not asserted alone above them. In our protocol's language this belongs to the *imago
dei*: a person known truly by those they trust, and protected by being known.

## What we have not solved

People whose only device is a hosted gateway need a home of their own for this model before they
can fully benefit from it; until then they get the simpler signals (device, place and time) and not
the intimate ones. The model must be guarded as carefully as a private key, because a stolen copy
would make imitation easier. And the line between witnessing and watching has to be drawn again
and again, in public. We would rather name these problems now than discover them in the market.

## Our ask

The race for intimacy assumes intimacy must be harvested to be useful. We are betting on the
opposite: that intimacy is most useful, and safest, when it never leaves the person it belongs to.
We would welcome your scrutiny of that bet, especially where you think it fails, and we would be
glad to share our designs as they mature.

With gratitude for the warning, and in hope of a better answer to it,

Matthew Dowell
Steward of the Elohim Protocol
