**Movement I — Waking Up · Day 0**

*Arc: from assuming the church stands outside the crisis → to honest lament that we are inside the attention economy.*

*Anchor text: Psalm 13 · Supporting: Deuteronomy 6:4-9; Jeremiah 17:9-11; Matthew 14:22-31 · Story: Exodus 1–2*

This opening session does one thing: it lets the crisis become real — not as an abstraction out there, but as something already shaping our habits, our households, and our churches. Definitions and diagnosis wait for Module 3. Today we see, we feel, and we learn the Bible's first faithful response to systemic harm: lament.

## Opening — A Liturgy of Lament

Begin not with information but with honesty before God. Read Psalm 13 responsively:

> How long, LORD? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me? How long must I wrestle with my thoughts...? (vv. 1-2)

Name it plainly: we live in a world engineered to wrestle our thoughts away, and most of us are losing. The psalmist gives us permission to tell God the truth about that — without pretending it is fine, and without despair. Hold a full minute of silence (notice how hard sixty undistracted seconds has become), then pray: *Lord, you search the heart. Show us today what has captured ours.*

## Humane Tech Concepts in View

Day 0 pairs with a screening of *The Social Dilemma* rather than an FHT module. Three ideas carry the session:

- **The attention economy** — human focus commodified for profit; "the race to the bottom of the brain stem." If you are not paying for the product, your attention is the product.
- **The Ledger of Harms** — CHT's running account of the costs: addiction, social isolation, outrage, misinformation, polarization — one interconnected system of *human downgrading*, not separate problems.
- **The AI dilemma** — the race has moved from attention to intimacy: systems now competing to form a personal relationship with you.

The full metacrisis taxonomy — the crisis pattern beneath the crises — (god-like technology, truth decay, institutional degradation, the broken epistemic commons) is deliberately deferred — that is Module 3's work. Today's claim is smaller and sharper: *this is already in our pews.*

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

1. Describe the attention economy's business model and the Ledger of Harms as portrayed in *The Social Dilemma*.
2. Identify where the race for attention already touches their own weekly habits and their congregation's life.
3. Contrast what Scripture binds to our hands and foreheads (Deuteronomy 6) with what our devices bind there now.
4. Practice biblical lament as the church's first faithful response to systemic harm — refusing both denial and despair.
5. Commit to the class covenant that will govern how this cohort learns together.

## Teaching

### Introduction: The Church Dilemma

We have lived through a technological revolution on the scale of the printing press, radio, and television — compressed into fifteen years and accelerating. The Church finds itself at a crossroads: called to be in the world but not of it (John 17:14-16), yet immersed in a digital landscape reshaping human behavior, relationships, and our shared sense of reality. Jesus said, "Where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (Matthew 6:21). In our age we must also ask: where your *attention* is, there your heart will be also.

The dilemma is manifold, and it previews the whole course:

- **Attention and discipleship** — how do we form deep disciples in a world engineered for distraction? (Movement II)
- **Community and connection** — why does more connectivity produce shallower belonging, and what would Acts 2:42-47 fellowship require of us? (Movements II & IV)
- **Truth and discernment** — how do we stay shrewd as snakes and innocent as doves (Matthew 10:16) in a distorted information ecosystem? (Movement IV)
- **Mission, justice, and stewardship** — how do we pursue the Great Commission, Micah 6:8 justice, and faithful stewardship of creation through tools we did not design and do not control? (Movements III–V)

Our goal — "see to it that no one takes you captive" (Colossians 2:8) — is not to be captivated by technology, but to captivate technology for Christ's purposes — and everyone, inside the church and out, agrees something is wrong. This course exists to recover mindfulness and agency: to upgrade our discipleship and collective wisdom for a crisis moment.

### Scripture Engagement

**Psalm 13 — the anchor.** Lament is not a loss of faith; it is faith refusing to lie. "How long must I wrestle with my thoughts?" is now a question with a business model attached. The psalm teaches the movement we will practice all course: honest complaint (vv. 1-2), petition (vv. 3-4), and the turn — "But I trust in your unfailing love" (v. 5). We lament *toward* hope.

**Deuteronomy 6:4-9 — Israel's attention technology.** The Shema commands God's words be bound on hands and foreheads — the tefillin were deliberately engineered reminders, a designed environment keeping love of God central to daily life. We still bind a device to our hands and hold it before our eyes dozens of times a day. The question is not *whether* we will wear an attention technology. The question is *whose*.

**Jeremiah 17:9-11 — the exploitable heart.** "The heart is deceitful above all things... who can understand it?" Persuasive technology is, precisely, an industry built on understanding it better than we do — and the partridge hatching eggs she did not lay names every empire built on riches without justice.

**Matthew 14:22-31 — attention amid the storm.** Peter walks on water while his attention is on Jesus; he sinks when the wind captures it. Our storm is engineered — outrage and fear are the weather of the feed. But note the gospel in the middle of it: *immediately* Jesus reached out his hand. Sinking is not the end of the story.

### Story: A People Inside a System

Exodus 1–2 shows Israel enslaved in a system they did not choose — their labor extracted to build someone else's empire, their thriving treated as a threat to be managed. Deliverance does not begin with a program. It begins with a groan: "The Israelites groaned in their slavery and cried out... God heard their groaning" (Exodus 2:23-24). The exile repeats the pattern — Jerusalem falls, and the people learn to sing lament in a strange land, and lament becomes the seedbed of renewal. This is our posture on Day 0: we are inside systems of extraction we did not individually choose. The first faithful act is not a solution. It is a groan God hears.

## Discussion & Activity

After the screening (or with it fresh from homework), work in three rounds:

1. **Ledger walk.** Post the Ledger of Harms categories around the room. Participants place sticky notes under each: *where did you see yourself? where did you see our church?* Cluster and read the patterns aloud.
2. **Discussion.** What are some ways the church is reacting to this change? What are some examples of "god-like" technology? What are the things churches do that make sense if we look for a race for attention?
3. **Communal lament.** In small groups, draft one sentence of honest complaint in the pattern of Psalm 13 ("How long, Lord..."). Gather the sentences into a single communal lament and pray it together. Do not resolve it. The turn toward hope comes — but let today's session end where Israel's deliverance began.

## The Class Covenant

Reframed from course rules into commitments we make to one another:

1. **We acknowledge the crisis** — social, cultural, economic, ecclesial — and that it is connected.
2. **We come as patients and participants, not spectators.** Churchgoers have been shaped as consumers, and the church, like media, sits downstream of the technology and incentives shaping culture.
3. **We refuse both denial and despair.** Lament is our doorway to hope, not our destination.
4. **We extend grace and refuse scapegoats.** Ministry is a marathon in a church filled with sprinters; we will not blame individuals for systemic failures (Module 2 gives us the lens for this).
5. **We aim at agency and mission** — equipping Christians and ministry leaders to re-present Christ in a world facing god-like technology, as builders and not only users.

## Application — Three Lanes

No fixing yet. This week's discipline in every lane is *seeing*.

- **Tech workers:** Write down what your product or team actually optimizes for. Note one place this week where the metric and love of the user diverge. Bring the note; you will use it in Modules 6, 8, and 9.
- **Households:** Run a one-week attention audit. When do screens appear at the table, in the car, in the first and last thirty minutes of the day? Observe without legislating — the household conversation comes in Movement II.
- **Church leaders:** Pull one week of your church's communications and ask honestly: what do these compete for — attention or formation? Notice where Sunday's messages are filtered through what the audience will tolerate hearing. Hold the findings for Modules 3 and 7.

## Conclusion

The biologist E.O. Wilson named our condition: Paleolithic emotions, medieval institutions, god-like technology. The race to the bottom of the brain stem produces addiction, isolation, outrage, misinformation, and polarization — not five problems but one interconnected system of human downgrading. The church has not been exempt; it has been blindsided. Social media broke the epistemic community of Christians much as it broke journalism, education, and family life — which raises the question this whole course exists to answer: *How can we attend the same church, live in the same city, read the same Bible, share the same Gospel — and end the day with such wildly different convictions about what it all means in practice?*

Today we only lamented, and that was the right work. But Psalm 13 does not end at verse 2 — "But I trust in your unfailing love; my heart rejoices in your salvation." The threat can be reversed: god-like technology can be redesigned to protect human vulnerability and mend the social fabric, as reimagined in the Kingdom of God. To respond without scapegoating, though, we need a new way of seeing. That is the next session's work: **Module 2 — Systems Thinking.**
