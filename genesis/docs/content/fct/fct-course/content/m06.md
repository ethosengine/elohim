**Movement II — Turning · Day 4**

*Arc: from counting only intended benefits → to counting the unpriced costs borne by neighbor and creation.*

*Anchor text: Luke 10:25-37 · Supporting: Genesis 2:15; Luke 14:28-33; Deuteronomy 31:14-23; Micah 6:8 (refrain) · Story: Joseph and the seven years (Genesis 41)*

Yesterday we reclaimed attention as sacred; today the turning widens. Captured attention is not only a private loss — everything we build and buy casts costs onto people who never agreed to pay them. Economists call these externalities. Scripture has an older word for the people who absorb them: *neighbors*. This module pairs with FHT's *Minimizing Harmful Consequences, Part 1* — the counting; Module 7 takes up the cure.

## Opening — Love Mercy

The refrain returns. Read Micah 6:8 together, and rest today on its second clause: *love mercy*. Mercy is the discipline of seeing costs that fall on others — the ones our balance sheets are designed not to show. Pray: *Lord, you see every sparrow and every moderator, every miner, every child downstream of what we build and buy. Give us eyes for the whole receipt, and hearts that love mercy more than margin.*

## Humane Tech Concepts in View

- **Externalities** — costs of an activity that land on parties outside the transaction. In technology they accumulate at scale: individually invisible, collectively civilization-shaping.
- **The imbalance of profitability.** Companies become wildly profitable by moving out of balance: toward extraction and away from stewardship; toward efficiency and away from resilience; toward competition and away from collaboration. The humane skill is knowing when each pole creates value and when it destroys it.
- **Tragedy of the commons and arms races** — the system shapes (from Modules 2–3) by which rational individual moves produce collective ruin; externalities are their exhaust.
- **Counting tools** — the Externality Rubric and Identifying Externalities worksheet; red-teaming and anti-KPIs as early-warning instruments; spotting harms in the design phase rather than the lawsuit phase; and the recognition that success needs measures beyond GDP and growth.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

1. Define externalities and explain how technology interacting with social structures produces them at civilization scale.
2. Apply the Identifying Externalities worksheet to a product, ministry, or household pattern, surfacing costs the current accounting hides.
3. Interpret the Good Samaritan as externality theology: the neighbor question answered as the cost question.
4. Connect Genesis 2:15 stewardship and Luke 14's count-the-cost discipleship to full-cost accounting as a spiritual discipline.
5. Cultivate prophetic foresight — Joseph's storehouses, Moses' song of witness — that anticipates harm before it lands.
6. Evaluate externalities generated by church practices themselves, using documented case studies with honesty and grace.

## Teaching

### Introduction: The Whole Receipt

Every transaction prints two receipts. One lists what we paid; the other — the one nobody prints — lists what everyone else paid: the attention of a teenager three time zones away, the wages of a content moderator, an aquifer, a downtown, a family dinner. Wild profitability is very often the art of maximizing the second receipt while displaying only the first. Minimizing harmful consequences begins as an accounting discipline: learn to see the whole receipt. That is not cynicism; it is mercy with a spreadsheet.

### Scripture Engagement

**Luke 10:25-37 — the anchor.** The lawyer asks Jesus the externality question of all time: "who is my neighbor?" — which is to say, *where may I stop counting?* Jesus answers with a man lying in the road: the absorbed cost of a system everyone else is moving through. The robbers extracted from him directly; the priest and Levite externalize him — his suffering is real but off their books, and they pass by on the other side, the very posture of externalized cost. The Samaritan does full-cost accounting: he sees, he stops, he pays out of his own pocket ("whatever more you spend, I will repay"). Jesus' closing inversion is the module's thesis: the question is not "who counts as my neighbor?" but "to whom will I *be* a neighbor?" — not where may I stop counting, but where will I start paying.

**Genesis 2:15 with Luke 14:28-33 — stewardship counts the cost.** Adam is placed in the garden "to work it and take care of it" — cultivation coupled to keeping, the original balance of efficiency and stewardship. And Jesus makes cost-counting a mark of discipleship: which of you, building a tower, does not first sit down and count the cost? The church has read Luke 14 as counting the cost *to ourselves* of following Jesus. This module extends the same discipline outward: no tower — no platform, no program, no campaign — without counting its full cost, including the portion others will pay.

**Deuteronomy 31:14-23 — the song of witness.** At the edge of the land, God tells Moses plainly that prosperity will produce forgetting: "when they have eaten their fill and grown fat, they will turn to other gods." And God's countermeasure is remarkable — not a wall, but a *song*, taught in advance, "a witness for me against the Israelites," living in their mouths so that when the troubles come the truth is already on their lips. God institutionalizes an anti-KPI: a liturgy designed to testify against the community's own future extraction. Churches that build honest counter-testimony into their worship — lament, confession, the whole receipt read aloud — are following a design pattern God himself established.

### Story: Joseph and the Seven Years

Genesis 41 shows foresight as statecraft. Pharaoh's dreams — fat cows devoured by thin ones — are a forecast of a cost arriving fourteen years out. Joseph's interpretation would be worthless without administration: a fifth of every harvest stored through the years of plenty, so that when the famine lands, Egypt feeds itself and its neighbors. Read through this module's lenses: the famine is the externality no one wants to book during the boom; unmanaged plenty would be a tragedy of the commons (consume it all now, starve together later); and Joseph's storehouses convert competition over scarcity into provision for the surrounding nations. Stewardship here is not private virtue — it is systems design under prophetic foresight, and it blesses people far outside the palace.

## Discussion & Activity

1. **Activity — the worksheet.** Work the Identifying Externalities worksheet on a chosen product, ministry, or household pattern. For each stakeholder ask: what do they pay that never appears in our accounting — attention, trust, health, place, formation?
2. **Case studies — the church's own exhaust.** Externalities are not only Silicon Valley's. Consider two documented cases with honesty and grace: *Jesus Camp* (2006), where a formation model produced diminished empathy toward those of different beliefs — a cost paid in the church's capacity to love neighbor and be heard as witness; and *Shiny Happy People* (2023), where an authority system enabled hidden abuse — a cost paid by victims first, and then by every community now met with earned distrust, and by those who rejected faith altogether. Discuss: what did each system optimize for, and who absorbed the unpriced cost? What would the "song of witness" have sounded like in those communities?
3. **Where do we pass by?** Name one place our congregation currently moves to the other side of the road — a cost we know about and have arranged not to see.

## Application — Three Lanes

This is Part 1 — the counting week. Remedies come tomorrow; resist fixing before you have finished seeing.

- **Tech workers:** Run the Externality Rubric on something you actually ship. Draft one anti-KPI — a number that would warn you the product is succeeding at someone else's expense — and identify the earliest point in your development process where externalities could be caught. Bring the Module 1 metric-vs-love note; it likely names your first externality.
- **Households:** Print the whole receipt for one household routine — the commute, the delivery habit, the streaming default. Who else pays, in labor, attention, or place? Choose nothing yet; just read the receipt aloud as a family, Deuteronomy-style, and let it be a witness.
- **Church leaders:** Inventory one flagship ministry with the worksheet — the Sunday production, the building, the missions model. Count costs to members (volunteer burnout, formation displaced by performance), to the neighborhood (traffic, land use, a campus dark six days a week), and to the wider Body (the smaller congregations your growth draws from — counted fully in Module 7's remedies). Present the uncomfortable line items to leadership before any solutions are proposed.

## Conclusion

Like Joseph, we are called to steward with foresight — to book the famine during the feast. We have learned to read the whole receipt: extraction's costs to neighbors we never see, efficiency's costs to resilience, competition's costs to the collaborative fabric that everything else depends on. And we have let the discipline turn homeward, counting the church's own exhaust without flinching, because the song of witness is sung first about ourselves. But counting is not the destination. Mercy loved rightly moves from seeing costs to healing systems — from "stop digging" to planting something that regenerates. That reimagining — and the changed definition of success it demands — is tomorrow: **Module 7 — Regeneration & Reconciliation.**

Passivity, neutrality, and ignorance are the mechanism. Read it with Module 9 in view: the habits we live by, not the values we state, reveal the fruit of our spirit.
