**Movement III — Reordering Loves · Day 6**

*Arc: from the myth of neutral technology → to design and use flowing from the greatest commandment.*

*Anchor text: Matthew 22:36-40 · Supporting: John 13:34-35; Romans 13:8-10; Micah 6:7-8 (refrain) · Story: Bad victories (Matthew 16:25-27)*

Movement II ended with a throne emptied: growth, engagement, and attendance dethroned as definitions of success. Movement III fills the throne. Technology is not and cannot be neutral — every artifact embeds someone's values — so the only real question is *which* values, chosen by whom. Jesus answered the ranking question two thousand years before the first A/B test: love God entirely, love neighbor as yourself, and let everything else hang on those two.

## Opening — The Metrics God Refuses

Read the refrain with the verse we usually skip. Micah 6:7 first: "Will the LORD be pleased with thousands of rams, with ten thousand rivers of oil?" — the prophet rejecting impressive quantitative metrics of devotion before verse 8 names what God actually requires. The warning is explicit: religious life has always been tempted to substitute the countable for the commanded. Pray: *Lord, we have offered you our numbers — our attendance, our engagement, our rivers of oil. You have told us what is good. Reorder our loves until what you require is what we optimize.*

## Humane Tech Concepts in View

This module pairs with FHT's *Centering Values, Part 1*:

- **The myth of technological neutrality.** The larger and more profitable a company, the greater the cost of taking responsibility for its harms — so neutrality arguments multiply with scale. In reality every design choice expresses values; technology and human behavior shape each other reciprocally.
- **Human and societal influence.** Personal experience, financial pressure, social dynamics, and cultural norms all flow into what gets built. Watch the gap between a company's *stated* values and its *demonstrated* values — the ones legible in the product itself.
- **The limitations of metrics.** Over-reliance on metrics masks harm; quantitative objectivity is not ethical neutrality. Goodhart's Law names the trap: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. (Micah 6:7 said it about rams and oil first.)

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

1. Refute the myth of technological neutrality, explaining how values and societal conditions shape every artifact.
2. Articulate a working definition of love from Matthew 22:36-40 and John 13:34-35 — personal and systemic, active and sacrificial, reconciling — robust enough to function as a design principle.
3. Evaluate current metrics in church and tech settings (attendance, engagement, retention) against the command to love, identifying alignment and conflict.
4. Analyze data collection, privacy, and algorithmic decision-making through the lens of "love does no harm to a neighbor" (Romans 13:10).
5. Distinguish stated from demonstrated values in an organization they know well, including their own congregation.
6. Create one love-centered modification to their personal, professional, or congregational technology practice.

## Teaching

### Introduction: Love, Defined for Design

If love is going to function as the centered value — evaluating everything from personal device habits to global platforms — it needs a definition sturdier than sentiment. Scripture supplies one with five load-bearing features:

1. **Personal and systemic at once.** The Greatest Commandment is two-fold: toward God (heart, soul, mind) and toward neighbor (as yourself). Every technological choice must answer for both relationships — what it does to communion with God, and what it does to tangible neighbors.
2. **Active and sacrificial, not sentimental.** "As I have loved you, so you must love one another" (John 13:34) — Christ's self-giving is the reference implementation. Love in technology means genuinely serving human flourishing, at cost if necessary, rather than exploiting human weakness profitably.
3. **Fruit-bearing and measurable.** Love manifests as the fruits of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22-23) — which means it has observable outputs. (This becomes Module 9's whole subject.)
4. **Coupled to power.** "From everyone who has been given much, much will be demanded" (Luke 12:48) — Module 3's design principle, now grounded in love rather than mere fairness. Gaining the power of gods without the love and wisdom of God is self-destruction.
5. **Reconciling and unifying.** Jesus prays "that they may be one" (John 17:20-23): love builds systems that heal divides rather than monetize them. (Movement IV will build on this.)

### Scripture Engagement

**Matthew 22:36-40 — the anchor.** Asked to rank the commandments, Jesus names two and then makes the architectural claim: "On these two commandments depend all the Law and the Prophets." *Depend* is a systems word — love of God and neighbor is the root dependency of the entire moral codebase; every other rule is downstream and breaks without it. A church or a product can implement hundreds of the derived rules while the root dependency fails — which is precisely Matthew 23:23's woe from Module 2. Centering values is not adding love to the feature list. It is recognizing that everything else already depends on it.

**John 13:34-35 — love as the visible metric.** "By this all people will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another." Jesus designates love as the church's *observable* signature — the metric by which outsiders evaluate the system. Not doctrine held, not attendance counted: love demonstrated. Stated versus demonstrated values, straight from the founder.

**Romans 13:8-10 — the harm test.** "Love does no harm to a neighbor; therefore love is the fulfillment of the law." Here is love operationalized as a floor: whatever else a design does, it must not harm the neighbor it touches — their attention, their dignity, their data, their children. Galatians 5:14 and James 2:8 confirm the compression: the entire law, fulfilled in one decree. For the Christian technologist this is the first acceptance test every feature must pass.

### Story: Bad Victories

There are such things as bad victories for a Christian — ways of seeming to win that cost you your soul. There are ways of gaining power, and ways of gaining material security, that ruin you; not because power or security are evil, but because *the means you use to acquire them are not neutral*. Given the choice between behaving viciously and winning, or behaving righteously with a real chance of losing — for the Christian, it is better to lose. Jesus meant it: "whoever would save his life will lose it... for what will it profit a man if he gains the whole world and forfeits his soul?" (Matthew 16:25-27). The tech industry's neutrality myth is, at bottom, a theory of guiltless victory — the claim that the scoreboard is exempt from the means. Matthew 16 denies any such exemption, for platforms and for churches alike: growth won by exploiting human weakness is a bad victory, and the ledger Jesus keeps prices it correctly.

## Discussion & Activity

A three-part personal reflection, worked alone and then shared:

1. **Examine.** Identify three specific ways you currently use technology that may not fully align with Jesus' command to love God and neighbor. (Common honest answers: social media use that breeds comparison and envy; streaming that displaces prayer, study, and community; shopping habits that trade others' labor conditions and creation's health for convenience and price.)
2. **Modify.** How could you practically change or replace these practices? (Time limits with the freed hours given to face-to-face relationship; one streaming hour traded for study or service; researching ethical alternatives and buying locally even at some cost.)
3. **Create.** Brainstorm a new technology, system, or lifestyle change that would help you better love God and neighbor — as specific and practical as possible. The point of part 3: love is not only a filter on what exists; it is a generator of what should.

## Application — Three Lanes

- **Tech workers:** Run the stated-vs-demonstrated audit on your own product: what do the defaults, dark patterns, and data practices *demonstrate*, whatever the values page states? Apply Romans 13:10 as an acceptance test to data collection, privacy, and algorithmic decisions: does this harm the neighbor it touches? Then bring forward your Module 4 wisdom-feature sketch and Module 5 Design Guide map — this module gives them their organizing value.
- **Households:** Work the three-part reflection as a family, each member naming one misaligned practice and one modification. Frame it positively: the household is choosing what its technology will be *for* — and love, not scarcity, is the reason for every boundary set.
- **Church leaders:** Evaluate your congregation's actual metrics — attendance, giving, online engagement, program participation — against John 13:35: would an outsider watching our dashboards conclude that love is what we optimize? Then consider building something: one congregation-scale proposal from the v1 course imagines a "Love Your Neighbor" platform — members' skills and availability matched to concrete local needs (elderly assistance, childcare for single parents, language practice with immigrants), integrated with local businesses for ethical consumption, with rhythms of shared meals and service — technology that moves a church from passive attendance toward everyday, practical love. Prototype a slice of it, even on a spreadsheet.

## Conclusion

Technology always embeds values; the myth of neutrality is simply how the powerful decline responsibility for which ones. We have named our center: love of God and neighbor, defined sturdily enough to design with — active, sacrificial, fruit-bearing, power-coupled, reconciling — and guarded by Micah's warning against substituting rivers of oil for what the Lord requires. In a world gaining god-like power, we are called to supply what Schmachtenberger observes it lacks: love and wisdom of the same scale.

But a centered value needs instrumentation. If we refuse engagement metrics, what do we watch instead? Jesus gave us the answer in agricultural form: you will recognize them by their fruits. Tomorrow we build the dashboard: **Module 9 — Fruit as Metric.**
