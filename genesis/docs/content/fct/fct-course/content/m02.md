**Movement I — Waking Up · Day 0 (second session)**

*Arc: from blaming individuals → to seeing interconnected systems as the arena of stewardship and the Body of Christ.*

*Anchor text: 1 Corinthians 12:12-27 · Supporting: Matthew 13:31-32 with Zechariah 4:10; Romans 12:2 · Story: Josiah and the trap of Moloch (2 Kings 23:10)*

Module 1 ended in lament, and the covenant we made there included a promise: no scapegoats. This module makes that promise keepable. The harms we lamented are not primarily the work of bad individuals — they are the outputs of systems, and systems are something Scripture has always trained God's people to see. Learning to think in systems is not secular analysis bolted onto faith; it is stewardship, humility, and Body-of-Christ theology given a working vocabulary.

## Opening — The Earth Is the Lord's

Read Psalm 24:1 — "The earth is the LORD's, and everything in it, the world, and all who live in it" — and Romans 12:2. Everything interconnected belongs to God; and the "pattern of this world" we are told not to conform to is exactly that: a pattern, a system that shapes us until our minds are renewed. Pray: *Lord, give us humble eyes. Where we have blamed people, teach us to see patterns; where we see patterns, teach us our part in them; and renew our minds so we can test and approve your will.*

## Humane Tech Concepts in View

- **Complicated vs. complex systems.** Complicated systems (engines, watches, computers) have many parts but knowable rules — predictable, controllable, and therefore brittle. Complex systems (ecosystems, learning, human community) adapt continuously; every interaction cascades, prediction fails, and resilience emerges. Scripture holds both: the Tabernacle's exact specifications (Exodus 25–31) are complicated; the living church of Acts and the body David marvels at in Psalm 139 are complex.
- **How to engage each.** Complicated: sense, analyze, respond. Complex: *probe, sense, respond* — run small experiments from your best hypothesis, watch what changes, adapt. Jesus ministered this way, engaging the complex system of human society situation by situation, and gave us the complex-systems test for truth: "by their fruit you will recognize them" (Matthew 7:16).
- **Simulations and play** as ways to internalize system dynamics (the course's lab thread lives here).
- **Habits reflect systems.** Our habitual behaviors are the fingerprints of the systems we live inside — which means changed systems, not just willpower, change lives.
- **Practices of design:** learning from living systems — God's creation as the original design school — and good urbanism as care for the public commons.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

1. Distinguish complicated from complex systems, giving one biblical example of each, and match the right engagement technique to each.
2. Identify how Scripture grounds interconnectedness and stewardship — seeing systems work as a form of caring for what belongs to God.
3. Interpret the Body of Christ (1 Corinthians 12) as a complex system whose health lies in the relationships among diverse parts, not the optimization of any one part.
4. Analyze a destructive social equilibrium (the Moloch trap) using multipolar-trap reasoning, and evaluate Josiah's reform as a change to the game's payoffs.
5. Trace how one of their own habits reflects a system they live inside, and redesign the system rather than resolving to try harder.
6. Practice epistemic humility (Proverbs 11:2; James 4:6) as the posture complexity demands and Scripture commands.

## Teaching

### Introduction: Two Kinds of Systems, One Lord

A Swiss watch and a garden are both intricate, but they fail differently. Take a watch apart and reassemble it correctly and it works; a garden cannot be disassembled at all — it can only be tended. Much of our frustration with church and technology alike comes from managing gardens as if they were watches: applying procedures, org charts, and metrics (complicated-system tools) to formation, community, and trust (complex realities). The complex world demands the probe-sense-respond posture Jesus modeled — and demands humility, because in complex systems, optimizing each part does not optimize the whole. As systems practitioners put it, the work shifts from perfecting your own part to improving the relationships among the parts. That sentence could be a commentary on 1 Corinthians 12.

### Scripture Engagement

**1 Corinthians 12:12-27 — the anchor.** Paul's Body is a complex system by every criterion: diverse parts, mutual dependence, no part dispensable, suffering and honor propagating through the whole ("if one part suffers, every part suffers with it"). Notice Paul's design insight: God has arranged the parts and given *greater honor to the parts that lacked it* — the Body is engineered against the winner-take-all dynamics that extractive systems produce. The eye cannot say to the hand "I don't need you"; neither can the platform say it to the user, nor the megachurch to the small congregation.

**Matthew 13:31-32 with Zechariah 4:10 — the physics of small beginnings.** The mustard seed and "do not despise the day of small things" teach complex-systems growth: small, faithful inputs compounding into shelter for the birds of the air. Kingdom change is incremental, bottom-up, and patient — which is also how resilient systems are actually built.

**Romans 12:2 — deconforming.** "The pattern of this world" is a systems phrase: conformity is what systems produce in their participants by default. Transformation requires the renewing of the mind — a deliberate counter-formation — before we can even *perceive* God's will clearly. Systems thinking, for the Christian, is part of that renewal: learning to see the pattern is the first step in refusing to be pressed into it.

### Story: Josiah and the Trap of Moloch

2 Kings 23:10 records Josiah desecrating Topheth "so no one could use it to sacrifice their son or daughter in the fire to Molek." Read as a multipolar trap: many families likely knew the practice was abominable, yet social and religious pressure, the fear of losing the deity's favor, and the signaling cost of non-participation held the equilibrium in place. No family could unilaterally defect without paying a price, so a society continued doing what perhaps few of its members individually wanted. Josiah's reform broke the trap by changing the game itself — destroying the altar removed the option, and the public, top-down act reset what participation signaled. The application writes itself: our era has its own altars where children are fed to systems nobody individually chose — and escaping such traps requires changing payoff structures, not just persuading individuals. Sometimes that is regulation; sometimes it is a community, like the church, agreeing to defect *together*.

## Discussion & Activity

**Compare and contrast: which does Jesus prioritize?** Read Matthew 23:23 (tithing mint, dill, and cumin while neglecting justice, mercy, and faithfulness) beside Matthew 22:36-40 (the greatest commandments, on which all the Law and Prophets hang). Tithing spices is complicated-system religion — precise, auditable, complete-able. Justice, mercy, and love of God and neighbor are complex — never finished, always adaptive. Jesus doesn't abolish the complicated ("without neglecting the former") but he unmistakably subordinates it. Discuss: where does our church excel at the auditable and neglect the weightier, and why is the auditable so much more comfortable?

**Activity — Habit archaeology.** Each participant picks one habit they wish were different (personal or congregational). Trace the system producing it: what cues, incentives, defaults, and community patterns hold it in place? Then redesign one element of the *system* — not a resolution to try harder. Share designs in pairs; note how personal disciplines (prayer, Scripture, service) function as counter-systems, and how community sustains them (Hebrews 10:24-25; Daniel's daily prayer, Daniel 6:10; Jesus wielding Scripture against temptation, Matthew 4:1-11). New habits ultimately flow from new identity: the new creation of 2 Corinthians 5:17, Saul become Paul.

## Application — Three Lanes

- **Tech workers:** Practice moving between parts and whole: this week, map how one feature, algorithm, or metric you own affects users' wider lives and communities — the relationship work that simple metrics never capture. Consider whether investigating those relationships should become a formal part of your role, and remember James Clear's principle (*Atomic Habits*, on tomorrow's shelf): outcomes follow systems, not intentions — so design the system that makes the good outcome natural.
- **Households:** Build identity-based habits as a family: name who you are in Christ, then install one small daily system that expresses it — a table liturgy, a screen-free hour, an evening examen. Trust the mustard seed: small and faithful beats grand and abandoned.
- **Church leaders:** See the church's own decline systemically. Much of what presents as spiritual decline was preceded by middle-class decline, individualized risk, and rising inequality squeezing the institutions that grew from healthier economic soil. Systemic responses belong on the church's agenda: transit, pedestrian, and bike access to campus and high-quality land use; pathways to household formation for young families; addressing real structural inequities in vocational training and higher education; job placement and career development as ministry. The church that tends the soil will not need to force the growth.

## Conclusion

We can now keep the covenant we made in lament: no scapegoats — because we see patterns, payoffs, and traps where we used to see only villains. We've learned that gardens aren't watches, that the Body's health lives in its relationships, that small faithful inputs compound, and that some evils persist not because everyone wants them but because no one can defect alone. Hold onto that last insight especially: it is the hinge of the whole course, and game theory has one more thing to teach us about it — in the long run, *we are each other's environment* (Module 4 will make that formal). First, though, seeing must become saying. Tomorrow we take everything these lenses reveal and state the problem precisely: **Module 3 — A Problem Well Stated.**
