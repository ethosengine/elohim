**Movement IV — Rebuilding Common Life · Day 8**

*Arc: from passive consumption of warped feeds → to disciplined discernment of how algorithms shape perception.*

*Anchor text: Ephesians 4:25 · Supporting: Exodus 20:16; Proverbs 6:16-19; 1 Corinthians 13:6; Amos 8:4-12 · Story: The click farms and the counterfeit Body*

Movement IV begins the rebuilding — and rebuilding starts with an honest survey of the damage. Social media is not a mirror of society but a funhouse mirror: the distortions of ourselves and of one another are the psycho-social externalities (Module 6's word) of engagement-maximizing business models. Before we can rebuild shared understanding, we must learn to name the specific ways the glass is bent.

## Opening — Love Rejoices with the Truth

Movement III gave us love as the center; Movement IV begins with love's relationship to truth. Read 1 Corinthians 13:6: "Love does not rejoice at wrongdoing, but rejoices with the truth." There is no love-without-truth option in the Christian life — and no truth-indifferent feed that love can inhabit comfortably. Pray: *Lord, you are the way, the truth, and the life. We have looked at ourselves and our neighbors in bent glass for a long time. Straighten our sight, and make us people whose love rejoices with the truth.*

## Humane Tech Concepts in View

This module pairs with FHT's *Creating Shared Understanding, Part 1 — The Breakdown*:

- **How the breakdown happens.** Engagement-driven algorithms shape perception at scale; society is a process, not a fixed thing, and information technology now disproportionately steers how it changes. The fragmentation has real national-security implications — a nation that cannot see together cannot act together.
- **The nine distortions** — a working taxonomy of the funhouse mirror: the *engaging content* distortion (what spreads ≠ what's true), *moral outrage* (indignation travels farthest), *amplification* (extremity rewarded), *extreme emotion* (calibrated provocation), *microtargeting* (each person shown a different reality), *othering* (out-groups flattened into caricature), *disloyalty* (nuance punished as betrayal by one's own tribe), *information flooding* (truth drowned rather than censored), and *anti-journalism* (the fourth estate's economics gutted).
- **The consequences:** degraded democratic processes, polarization and tribalism, and erosion of the cultural trust effective governance — and effective church life — requires.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

1. Explain how engagement-driven systems distort perception of self, neighbor, and society — the funhouse mirror rather than the mirror.
2. Identify each of the nine distortions in the wild, with examples from their own feeds and from Christian discourse specifically.
3. Relate the distortions to biblical categories: false witness, slander, discord-sowing, and the tongue as fire.
4. Evaluate how these distortions compromise the church's unity and its witness, including in their own congregation's media presence.
5. Assess their personal contribution to and formation by the breakdown of shared understanding.
6. Practice concrete social-media hygiene that defends attention and truth without retreating into isolation.

## Teaching

### Introduction: The Funhouse Mirror

C.S. Lewis observed in *Mere Christianity* that God can fully show himself only to people united in a body — loving one another, helping one another, showing him to one another, "like players in one band." That is what humanity was meant to be like; it is also, precisely, what the distortions attack. Social media platforms are not neutral observers of the Body: they poke and prod users toward profitable behaviors, constructing a distorted image of each user for the system — and, worse, distorted images of users *for one another*. Your feed's picture of your brother is not your brother. When millions of Christians relate to each other primarily through bent glass, the band cannot hear itself play.

### Scripture Engagement

**Ephesians 4:25 — the anchor.** "Having put away falsehood, let each one of you speak the truth with his neighbor, *for we are members one of another*." Paul grounds truth-telling not in abstract accuracy but in membership — the Body refrain doing new work. Falsehood between members is autoimmune disease: the body attacking its own tissue. Every distortion this module names is, for the church, a membrane failure in the one Body; and the command is not merely "don't lie" but the positive discipline of *speaking truth with* — direct, relational, unmediated by the outrage market.

**Exodus 20:16 with Proverbs 6:16-19 — false witness, industrialized.** The ninth commandment prohibits bearing false witness against your neighbor; the feed automates it — sharing the decontextualized clip, the misattributed quote, the flattened caricature of the other tribe is false witness at retweet speed. And Proverbs' list of seven things the Lord hates reads like an engagement playbook audit: a lying tongue, a heart devising wicked plans, feet quick to evil, a false witness breathing lies, and — the climactic seventh — *one who sows discord among brothers*. Discord-sowing is not a bug of the current information economy. It is the business model. James 3:5-6 supplies the physics: the tongue is a small fire that sets forests ablaze — and we have given the spark a global wind machine.

**Matthew 12:36-37 — words on the ledger.** "I tell you, on the day of judgment people will give account for every careless word they speak, for by your words you will be justified, and by your words you will be condemned." Jesus attaches eternal accounting to *careless* words — not only malicious ones — and the immediate context is his tree-and-fruit teaching (12:33-35): speech is fruit, the overflow of the heart's stored treasure. In an economy that industrializes careless speech — the caption dashed off, the quote-post fired before reading, the share made on the headline alone — this may be the New Testament's most under-preached verse for our moment. Every share is testimony, and the ledger is kept.

**1 Corinthians 13:6 — the criterion.** Love rejoices with the truth. Any "Christian" content strategy that rejoices in wrongdoing — that monetizes outrage against neighbors — fails Movement III's test before it starts.

### The Composite Distortion: A Hypothesis on What Manufactures the Culture War

The nine distortions are mechanisms. Name now the formation they assemble into — and weigh a hypothesis about the generator that powers them all (Module 3's word). The hypothesis: behind the distortion machine runs **economic inequality**. Wilkinson and Pickett's data, on this module's shelf, find that more unequal societies show lower trust and higher status anxiety; their critics (Christopher Snowdon, Peter Saunders) answer that the correlations are fragile to which countries are counted and what else is controlled for, and whether inequality erodes trust or eroded trust permits inequality remains disputed. So hold it the way Module 9 taught: as a claim about fruit, to be inspected together — and the same test binds whichever tribe's explanation of the culture war you walked in with. What is harder to dispute is the material hollowing of the epistemic commons: local ad revenue extracted to a duopoly, newsrooms rolled up and stripped, half the nation's counties left in news deserts. And into that vacuum the attention economy sells the one conflict that is cheap to produce, infinitely renewable, and — decisively — safe for its owners: identity conflict. If the hypothesis holds, material grievance that cannot find honest expression gets *re-formed*, epistemically, into the **culture war**: a formation in which every question arrives pre-sorted into tribes and the ledger of who-is-taking-what never comes up. Whatever its fuel, call the phenomenon by its name. The culture war is not a disagreement the media covers; it is a product the distortion machine manufactures — sold to both tribes at once.

**Amos 8:4-12 — the generator, and the famine it ends in.** Scripture ran this causal chain twenty-seven centuries ago. The oracle indicts the economy first: "you who trample the needy... skimping the measure, boosting the price and cheating with dishonest scales" — economic exploitation and epistemic fraud in a single image, for crooked scales are both. And where does it land? Not in economic collapse but in epistemic catastrophe: "I will send a famine through the land — not a famine of food or a thirst for water, but a famine of hearing the words of the LORD. People will stagger from sea to sea... searching for the word of the LORD, but they will not find it." Inequality ends in a sensemaking famine. Amos names what our dashboards now invite us to test: a people that tolerates dishonest scales eventually loses the capacity to hear truth at all.

And here is the distortion that should grieve the church most: the culture war consumes the very voice that would have raised the economic question. The biblical prophets are relentlessly economic — Amos on scales, Isaiah on land consolidation ("woe to you who add house to house and join field to field," 5:8), James on withheld wages that "cry out against you" (5:4). A church discipled by the feed re-forms that prophetic office into culture-war chaplaincy — combat commentary for one tribe or the other — and goes silent on the one subject the prophets never went silent on. Recognition is the first mercy: a manufactured formation can be declined. The refusal, and the rebuilding, is Module 11's work.

### Story: The Counterfeit Body

Imagine the bustling digital marketplace of faith: millions of Christians daily sharing prayers, verses, and testimonies — the global, interconnected Body we long envisioned. Then the sobering reality: a 2019 investigation found that 19 of the top 20 Christian Facebook pages were run not by ministries or believers but by foreign click farms, primarily in Kosovo and Macedonia. The operators had identified the faithful as a lucrative demographic: trusting, eager to engage spiritual content, quick to share what resonates — especially with a dose of political outrage stirred in. The content ranged from misattributed inspirational quotes to engineered fear and division; the purpose was always the same: capture and monetize the attention of believers.

Sit with the implications. The majority of "Christian" content forming your family and congregation was created to exploit, not edify. Authentic voices were drowned out by the flood (the *information flooding* distortion, aimed at the church). Believers' understanding of their own faith was subtly warped by algorithmically optimized pseudo-faith, and the watching world's picture of Christianity was warped with it. Our genuine hunger for spiritual connection was weaponized against us. This is Matthew 10:16 territory — the wolves came dressed not as sheep but as *shepherd content* — and it is why shrewdness is not optional and discernment is not paranoia. Let righteous anger fuel the constructive work: discerning consumption, responsible sharing, support for authentic ministries, and — for those with the skills — building the alternative.

## Discussion & Activity

1. **The church's own funhouse.** Think of church "experiences" you have worked on, supported, or attended. Where might they have contributed to or amplified any of the nine distortions — engaging-content pressure on sermon clips, moral outrage in the church's social feeds, othering of those outside?
2. **Design levers.** What design features increase distortion in shared understanding, and what features combat it? Draw on every prior module's toolkit.
3. **Personal audit.** How have I contributed to, and been formed by, the breakdown of shared understanding? What one step would foster greater understanding in my digital interactions this week?
4. **Principles.** What principles should guide our church's use of social media and digital platforms — written down, not assumed?

## Application — Three Lanes

- **Tech workers:** Take the nine distortions to work as a red-team checklist: which does your product structurally reward, even unintentionally? Prototype one counter-distortion feature — friction before sharing, context before outrage, provenance made visible. Deepen your platform knowledge: you cannot design against dynamics you cannot name.
- **Households:** Adopt the hygiene disciplines together: an intentional media diet anchored in reputable local sources; verification before sharing (make "did we check this?" a family reflex); emotional checking — pausing to notice anger or fear before engaging; regular digital fasting; and privacy practices that shrink the surface available for microtargeting. Model mindful engagement: be the voice of reason and empathy you wish the feed had.
- **Church leaders:** Make digital literacy a discipleship ministry — fact-checking, algorithmic awareness, and misinformation recognition taught as seriously as financial stewardship. Audit the church's own content strategy against the distortions (are we clipping sermons for outrage-adjacent engagement?). And take the plank out first: before the church can offer epistemic hope to the world, it must become a truthful, informed people itself.

## Conclusion

These distortions have run loose for over a decade, and under them the unity of the Body is genuinely threatened: platforms carve congregations into warring tribes, the most engaged voices grow most extreme, peacemakers are punished as disloyal, and those who opt out of the culture war often end up isolated — the opposite of unity in diversity. The click-farm revelation is the wake-up call: even our faith can be counterfeited by the attention economy. If the church cannot rebuild the epistemic commons needed for one Body under one Head, it has no truth-hope to offer a post-truth world.

But naming the distortions is already the turn toward repair. We are called to be agents of reconciliation (2 Corinthians 5:18-20) — and recognition without rebuilding would leave us merely well-informed cynics. Tomorrow the constructive work begins: the practices and structures by which a community makes sense of the world *together*. **Module 11 — Collaboration & Sensemaking.**

## Homework

Practice: **Reclaiming Your Sacred Attention on Social Media** (~75 minutes, then a follow-up). (1) *Audit your feed*: identify accounts that trigger strong emotion, exist mainly for engagement, or may spread misinformation. (2) *The great unfollow*: unfollow or mute at least five, noting why. (3) *Mindful following*: add five reputable organizations or experts, noting what each contributes. (4) *Rebuilding bridges*: prayerfully consider unblocking friends or family cut off over disagreements — we are called to unity — and plan how to engage more constructively. (5) *Reflect* (150–200 words) on what changed and what will be hard. One week later, spend ten minutes reviewing the difference. This is intentional attention, not isolation from diverse viewpoints.
