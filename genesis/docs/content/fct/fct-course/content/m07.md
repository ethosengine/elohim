**Movement II — Turning · Day 5**

*Arc: from extractive growth-at-all-costs church success → to regenerative, right-sized community.*

*Anchor text: Acts 2:42-47 (refrain home) · Supporting: Haggai 1:2-11; Matthew 6 · Lab: Parable of the Polygons (from Module 6's homework)*

This module closes Movement II by changing the paradigm of success itself. Modules 5 and 6 taught us to receive attention as sacred and to count unpriced costs; now repentance becomes constructive: what would church look like if it stopped extracting and started regenerating? The FHT answer — bind scale to responsibility, use externalities as design criteria, do less harm — turns out to be something the prophets and Jesus have been saying all along.

## Opening — Consider Your Ways

Read Haggai 1:5-6 aloud: "Consider your ways. You have sown much, and harvested little. You eat, but you never have enough... and he who earns wages does so to put them into a bag with holes." Sit with the image — an economy of maximum activity and minimum satisfaction, twenty-five centuries before the infinite scroll. Pray: *Lord of hosts, show us where we have paneled our own houses while your house — your people, your creation, our neighbors — lies in ruins. Give us courage to consider our ways.*

## Humane Tech Concepts in View

This module pairs with FHT's *Minimizing Harmful Consequences, Part 2 — Reimagining Systems*:

- **Regenerative vs. extractive systems** — economies (and institutions) that replenish their substrate versus those that consume it; Kate Raworth's Doughnut Economics as a framework for meeting human needs within real limits.
- **Bind scale to responsibility** — the paradigm shift away from growth at all costs: as reach grows, so must accountability. Practical tools: externalities as design criteria, red-teaming, anti-KPIs.
- **Do less harm before doing more tech** — once a system operates at unsustainably extractive scale, solving tech's problems with more tech usually compounds them. When you find yourself in a hole, stop digging.
- **Strengthening open societies** — building technology (and institutions) that improve collaborative problem-solving rather than degrade it.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

1. Distinguish regenerative from extractive systems and apply Doughnut Economics as a picture of right-sized community life.
2. Evaluate their own church's practices for extraction — where growth is subsidized by unpriced costs to members, neighbors, or the wider Body of Christ.
3. Interpret Haggai 1 and Acts 2:42-47 as contrasting economies: wages in a bag with holes versus a fellowship where no one lacked.
4. Apply "bind scale to responsibility" to church institutions — including exceeding legal minimums on misconduct, finances, and equity as institutional repentance that rebuilds trust.
5. Redefine church success beyond attendance and production value, and design one regenerative practice for resource use, community engagement, or technology adoption.
6. Articulate how a church can serve as a prophetic voice for regenerative approaches to technology in wider society.

## Teaching

### Introduction: Stop Digging

It's tempting to think all we need is more technology to solve our problems — hasn't technology solved so many already? But once we operate at extractive scale, addressing technology's problems with more technology tends to produce a larger set of problems. Minimizing harmful consequences begins with identifying the externalities growing outside our balance sheet — and, often, with doing less.

The Parable of the Polygons (last module's lab) left us three lessons that now become church lessons: small individual bias produces large collective bias, so naming a culture's failure is not an attack on its individuals; the past haunts the present — a floor doesn't stop being dirty just because you stopped dropping food, and creating equity is ongoing work like staying clean; and *demand diversity near you* — if everyone around you is a triangle, you are missing some amazing squares, and that's unfair to everyone. Regenerative solutions usually begin not with a new program but with an honest inventory of what we could stop doing.

### Scripture Engagement

**Acts 2:42-47 — the anchor, and the refrain's home.** Here is the destination picture the whole course keeps returning to: devotion to teaching, fellowship, breaking of bread, and prayer; possessions sold as any had need; day-by-day life together in temple and homes; glad and generous hearts — "and the Lord added to their number daily." Read it as an economy: right-sized, regenerative, zero unpriced costs offloaded onto the vulnerable. Notice what the passage does *not* attribute growth to: no attractional production, no campaign. Growth is the overflow of a community whose life together is itself the witness.

**Haggai 1:2-11 — the counter-economy.** "Is it a time for you yourselves to dwell in your paneled houses, while this house lies in ruins?" The people aren't idle — they've sown much. But extraction without regeneration harvests little: never enough food, never warm, wages in a bag with holes. Haggai names the spiritual physics of extractive systems: maximum throughput, minimum flourishing, and a commons (God's house) in ruins while private paneling gleams.

**2 Corinthians 8:12-15 — isotēs, the economics of enough.** Organizing famine relief, Paul names the goal: "your abundance at the present time should supply their need, so that their abundance may supply your need — that there may be *isotēs*" (fairness, balance). Then he grounds it in the manna economy: "whoever gathered much had nothing left over, and whoever gathered little had no lack." This is non-zero-sum theology with apostolic authority: resources circulate rather than accumulate, today's giver is tomorrow's receiver, and the success condition is universal sufficiency — not maximum accumulation. Isotēs is what Module 4's "possible win-wins" look like when the church runs the economy, and it is the standing indictment of platforms built on permanent extraction and artificial scarcity: hoarding what could be abundant, against the manna's plain warning.

**2 Kings 12 — the chest with the bored lid.** Haggai preaches the rebuild; Joash shows the mechanics. When the old system failed — priests collecting repair money that never repaired anything — the king installed a transparent one: a chest with a hole bored in its lid, set beside the altar, emptied under dual oversight, the royal secretary and the high priest counting together. Then the remarkable verse: the workmen received the money with no accounting required, "because they acted with complete honesty" (v. 15). Accountability at the system's top purchased trust at its base — a trust dividend three millennia before Trustville (Module 11). The coda instructs too: under threat from Hazael king of Aram, Joash stripped the temple treasures to buy peace. Even well-designed systems break under pressures leadership has not covenanted through. Institutional repentance needs both the chest and the courage.

### Story: Jesus' Economics of the Secret (Matthew 6)

Matthew 6 reads like Jesus red-teaming the metrics of religious life. Giving announced with trumpets, prayer performed on street corners, fasting with a disfigured face — each optimizes the visible metric and, Jesus says, thereby receives its full reward: the engagement was the payout, and there is nothing left. Against this he sets the economics of the secret: giving your left hand doesn't audit, prayer behind a closed door, treasure stored where moths and metrics can't reach. Then the choice beneath it all: "No one can serve two masters... you cannot serve both God and money" — or God and growth. And the anti-anxiety economics of the birds and lilies: provision without hoarding, beauty without labor-as-identity, "seek first his kingdom... and all these things will be given to you as well." Matthew 6 is the paradigm change this module asks of the church: success redefined from what is seen and counted to what the Father sees in secret.

### Counter-Story: The Sabbath Year Under Siege (1 Maccabees 6)

Regeneration has enemies, and the intertestamental record preserves the collision. During the Maccabean wars, Judea's defenders ran out of food at the worst possible moment "because it was the seventh year" — the sabbatical rest of the land (1 Maccabees 6:49-54). The regenerative rhythm God commanded had become a military liability under siege; the pressure to abandon the Sabbath year was not spiritual laziness but survival logic inside an arms race. Name the pattern honestly, because it is ours: the attention economy, like the Seleucid empire, needs your surplus — and it punishes every community that tries to keep an economics of enough. The question this module leaves with the church is the Maccabean question: can we sustain an alternative economic logic while embedded in a system built to break it? That is exactly why regeneration must be communal (Acts 2), designed (the chest), and covenanted (Module 15's manifesto): no household keeps Sabbath economics alone under siege.

## Discussion & Activity

Central question: **How can churches embody Christ's vision of regeneration and reconciliation in their use and development of technology, while avoiding extractive systems and harmful externalities?** Work it in rounds:

1. **Extraction inventory.** Where does our church's operating model consume its own substrate — volunteer burnout, debt service, energy, the trust eroded by seeker-friendly theological thinness? At what point would *we* become Mars Hill — the Seattle megachurch whose rise and collapse Module 1's podcast shelf documents — so leveraged and complicated that one crisis ends us?
2. **Anti-KPIs for church.** If Matthew 6 is Jesus red-teaming visible metrics, draft two anti-KPIs for your congregation — numbers you commit to *not* optimizing, and what you would watch instead.
3. **Growth audit.** If our growth comes from poaching better than smaller congregations can, is the Body of Christ actually growing? What would success measured at the level of the whole Body look like?

## Application — Three Lanes

- **Tech workers:** Take "externalities as design criteria" into your actual backlog: propose one red-team exercise or anti-KPI for a product you touch. And if your industry itself generates externalities you can no longer square with your faith, know that vocation realignment is a legitimate act of repentance — one your church should support without penalty or disruption (see the church-leader lane). Then run the Isotēs Checklist on something you ship: does it create reciprocal abundance (users' surplus meeting others' needs, and returning), temporal contingency (today's giver can be tomorrow's receiver), and universal sufficiency — or permanent winners and losers, extracting from and manufacturing scarcity out of what could be abundant?
- **Households:** Apply "do less": choose one consumption pattern to shrink rather than optimize. Practice the Polygons lesson at home — demand diversity near you; make active choices about who is at your table rather than passively hoping proximity happens.
- **Church leaders:** Bind your scale to responsibility. (1) *Universal-Body growth*: treat other congregations' flourishing as your metric too — share what you do well, and let planting, building, or relocating regenerate the places you leave. (2) *Operational regeneration*: right-size buildings and debt, run on renewable energy, design for walking/biking/transit, extend the church's capacity into health care, social services, and income stabilization. (3) *Institutional repentance* (received from Module 4): exceed legal minimums on sexual misconduct, tax law, and equity — racial and class — boldly and publicly; doing more than required is how broken institutions rebuild trust. (4) *Outcome-honest discernment*: we cannot be a community serving the poor, prisoners, immigrants, orphans, and widows while supporting, from whichever side of the aisle, what measurably leaves those same neighbors poorer, more endangered, or more alone — we cannot go on sinning that grace may abound. Judge every political commitment the congregation holds, whatever its direction, by measurable outcomes, not stated goals — and inspect your own side's fruit first. (5) *Vocation support*: build ministry capacity that helps members realign their work with their values.

## Conclusion — Movement II Closes

The turning is complete. We received attention as sacred (Module 5), learned to count what our systems cost our neighbors (Module 6), and now we have repented of the growth paradigm itself — trading the trumpet for the secret, the paneled house for the commons, extraction for regeneration. The Acts 2 picture is no longer nostalgia; it is a design specification: a right-sized fellowship whose economy runs on glad and generous hearts, to which the Lord adds. Movements of Christians are already building toward it — from new monastics to parish-rooted communities to creation-justice congregations (see Go Deeper) — communities that see themselves as part of the ecosystem they are healing, not outside saviors.

But repentance empties a throne, and something must be enthroned in its place. If engagement, attendance, and growth no longer define success — what does? Movement III answers: the greatest commandment, deliberately centered. **Module 8 — Centering Love.**
