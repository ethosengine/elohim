Jesus' banquet parable will not allow it — the master's order is not "open the doors" but "go out... compel them to come in, so that my house will be full." ## Opening — Act Justly

The refrain reaches its hardest clause. Read Micah 6:8 — *to act justly* — and let Amos supply the image: "let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" (Amos 5:24). Not a trickle of charity; a river that reshapes the landscape. Pray: *Lord of the banquet, you have shown us what is good. We have walked humbly and learned to love mercy; now give us the courage to act justly — to go out to the roads and country lanes, and not to rest while there is still room in your house.*

## Humane Tech Concepts in View

This module pairs with FHT's *Supporting Fairness and Justice*:

- **The Capability Approach** (Nussbaum and Sen) — justice measured not by rights declared but by people's real capability to exercise them: life, health, thinking, affiliation, control over one's environment. Opportunity made actual, not theoretical.
- **Checks, balances, and humility** — the tech industry's asymmetric power over users demands accountability structures; "technological solutionism" is the industry's version of pride.
- **Unintended consequences and edge cases** — convenience for the center routinely becomes burden at the margins; who gets treated as an "edge case" is itself a justice decision.
- **Algorithmic bias** — systems inherit and amplify societal bias: facial recognition, résumé screening, predictive policing. Weaponization of platforms against vulnerable communities; content moderation that fails the global margins first.
- **Algorithmic management and inequality** — software as boss, eroding workers' agency; technology compounding economic divides.
- **The humane posture:** anticipate harm, build real relationships with vulnerable users, include diverse perspectives in design, and support those already working for justice.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

1. Explain the Capability Approach and connect it to biblical justice (*mishpat*) as enacted participation — faith that does, not merely says (Matthew 7:21; James 2:17).
2. Interpret the Great Banquet as a divine imperative for *active* inclusion: urgency ("compel them"), abundance ("still room"), and boundary-crossing (roads and country lanes).
3. Analyze algorithmic bias through the doctrine of fallenness, and describe what "redeeming" an algorithm requires in concrete cases.
4. Apply Imago Dei and divine impartiality (Acts 10:34-35) to design decisions about edge cases, moderation, and vulnerable communities.
5. Develop a "Digital Jubilee" vision from Leviticus 25 — periodic, structural restoration of access, literacy, and opportunity.
6. Evaluate their own lifestyle and congregational practices for the ways charity can insulate rather than transform, and chart the move from feeling generous to acting justly.
7. Read the biblical tithe and the sabbatical release (Deuteronomy 14–15) as a structural floor for the poor, and evaluate whether their congregation's teaching on giving takes account of its members' *real* finances: not only what each household earns and owes, but how it lives compared with its median neighbor.

## Teaching

### Introduction: Justice as Enacted Participation

Justice, in its fullest sense, is the degree to which people can actually participate in and benefit from the goods we claim to hold dear. Declaring rights is cheap; justice arrives when people have the genuine capability to exercise them. This is the Capability Approach — and it is also, strikingly, the Bible's grammar of faith. "Not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the kingdom... but the one who *does* the will of my Father" (Matthew 7:21). "Faith by itself, if it is not accompanied by action, is dead" (James 2:17). "These people honor me with their lips, but their hearts are far from me" (Matthew 15:8). The fruit test from Module 9 now grades our systems: are we creating digital and civic ecosystems in which every image-bearer can actually flourish — or declaring fairness while designing exclusion?

### Scripture Engagement

**Luke 14:15-24 — the anchor.** Jesus' banquet parable carries three imperatives for builders. First, **divine urgency for inclusion**: the master doesn't post an invitation — "Go out to the roads and country lanes and compel them to come in." Active seeking, not passive accessibility. Second, **abundance**: "'what you ordered has been done, but there is still room.'" Empty seats are not neutral; unused capacity is a standing indictment, and fullness — "so that my house will be full" — is the success metric. Third, **boundary-breaking**: the guest list inverts the social order ("the poor, the crippled, the lame, the blind," 14:13-14 — invited precisely because they cannot repay). Applied to technology: move from access to *agency*; seek out the digitally marginalized rather than waiting; and measure success by who is finally at the table, not who was theoretically welcome.

**Acts 10:34-35 — impartiality as divine character.** "God shows no partiality." Peter's hardest lesson — crossing the deepest boundary he knew — is the theological ground for edge-case-first design: any system that works well only for people like its designers embeds a partiality God does not share.

**Leviticus 25 — Jubilee.** Israel's economy included a structural reset: every fiftieth year, land returned, debts released, the accumulating machinery of inequality deliberately interrupted. Jubilee assumes drift toward concentration and legislates against it. A *Digital Jubilee* would do the same for our age's compounding exclusions: access, devices, literacy, and opportunity periodically and deliberately restored, not left to trickle.

**Deuteronomy 14:28-29 and 15:1-11: the tithe was a floor, and "the poor you will always have" is a command.** Much preaching on the tithe stops at Malachi 3. Deuteronomy says what it was *for*. In most years the tithe of the harvest was eaten in a feast before the Lord by the household that brought it, "you and your household, rejoicing" (14:26). Every third year it was stored in the towns "so that the Levite, the foreigner, the fatherless and the widow… may come and eat and be satisfied" (14:28-29). The tithe was a celebration and a safety net, paid out of the *produce of the land*, which in an agrarian economy meant out of capital. It was not a flat tax on wages to pay for a building. The next chapter cancels debts every seventh year and makes a promise: "there need be no poor people among you" (15:4). Then it gives the line Jesus later quoted: "There will always be poor people in the land. *Therefore* I command you to be openhanded toward your fellow Israelites who are poor and needy" (15:11). When "the poor you will always have with you" (Mark 14:7) is used to excuse indifference, it reverses its own source. In Deuteronomy it is the reason for open hands and debt release. Jesus opened his ministry the same way, reading Isaiah: "good news to the poor… freedom for the prisoners… to proclaim the year of the Lord's favor" (Luke 4:18-19), which is Jubilee language.

Scripture names the matching sin as consistently as any economic sin it names, in every genre: in the law (Exodus 22:25; Leviticus 25:35-37; Deuteronomy 23:19-20), in Nehemiah's rebuke of the nobles (Nehemiah 5), in the Psalms (15:5), and in Ezekiel's list of what a righteous man does not do (18:8, 13). That sin is *usury*, profit taken from a neighbor's need. It is one of the most under-preached sins in the American church. Its modern forms reach a worker before the first paycheck arrives: rent on land whose value the whole community created, interest on debts taken on to survive, platform tolls, data harvested without pay. Even the New Testament passages most often used to police modesty name *wealth on display*, "braided hair or gold or pearls or expensive clothes" (1 Timothy 2:9; 1 Peter 3:3). This is not license to stop giving. Giving is worship, and churches need support. It is a call to give and to *teach* giving the way Deuteronomy does: as a floor under the poorest family in the room, not a weight on it.

**Isaiah 65, Micah 4 and Revelation 21–22: where the economy is going.** Scripture's economy begins in a garden (Module 7) and ends in a garden-city, and the ending reverses each loss. Labor is no longer taken by someone else: "They will build houses and dwell in them… no longer will they build houses and others live in them" (Isaiah 65:21-22). Everyone has enough and is safe: "Everyone will sit under their own vine and under their own fig tree, and no one will make them afraid" (Micah 4:4), sufficiency held in security, not accumulation, right after swords are beaten into plowshares. The curse on work is lifted ("No longer will there be any curse," Revelation 22:3), and the tree of life bears fruit every month, its leaves "for the healing of the nations" (22:2). Fruit that comes every month needs no barns. And "on no day will its gates ever be shut" (21:25): the opposite of the gated suburb. Revelation also names the counter-city. Babylon's merchants mourn a cargo list that ends with "human beings sold as slaves" (18:13), an economy whose abundance required someone's poverty. The tithe as a floor, the release of debts, a wage no one withholds: each is a way of living in the second city before it arrives. Measuring the gap tells us which city we are living in.

**Micah 6:8 — act justly.** The refrain's remaining clause, now due. Humility (Module 4) and mercy (Module 6) without action is the banquet announced and never filled.

### Story: Dignity Written into Law

In the rubble of post-war Germany, an extraordinary experiment asked whether a society could be rebuilt so that its worst history could not recur. The 1949 Basic Law answered by putting one sentence first — Article 1: "Human dignity shall be inviolable. To respect and protect it shall be the duty of all state authority" — and then building institutions that made the sentence load-bearing: a social state committed to basic needs, healthcare as a right rather than a privilege, robust public broadcasting so citizens could know enough to govern themselves, and a criminal justice system oriented to rehabilitation, with the lower recidivism to show for it. The lesson for Kingdom Developers: justice does not simply happen. It flows from how a people *chooses* to live together — from systems intentionally designed so that dignity is not aspiration but architecture. We are at a comparable design moment for the digital world, and the same question is on the table: will human dignity be inviolable in what we build?

## Discussion & Activity

Sit with the uncomfortable reflection: consider your morning routine — the coffee, the commute, the neighborhood — and then the homeless person you passed on the way here. How do our lifestyle choices — where we live, how we move, what we consume — contribute to the economic systems that produce homelessness? In what ways do our feelings of generosity when we give to church programs actually *insulate* us from confronting the impact of our daily decisions? How do we move beyond feeling good about occasional charity to choices that promote justice in everyday life?

Then turn the same question on the pulpit. Look up current median household wealth for your country (Politizane's *Wealth Inequality in America* videos are a vivid place to start) and assume, as is almost certainly true, that your congregation has no billionaires. Roughly half the families in the room are then just getting by, and many owe more than they own. What would it sound like to preach the tithe to *them* in Deuteronomy's terms, as the floor that holds them up and not a charge on what little they have? Does your leadership know what is happening in members' finances, and does it believe that is its concern?

Last, widen what counts as "finances." Real finances are not only what we earn and what we owe. They include **the gap**: how we live compared with our median neighbor, and whose hours our margin depends on. The rich fool in Luke 12 had a surplus and a plan for bigger barns, and God called him a fool because he was "not rich toward God." Many churches teach household finance through programs like Dave Ramsey's *Financial Peace University* and its companion book *The Total Money Makeover*, and many people in the room will know them well. Name honestly what such programs get right: debt is a real bondage ("the borrower is the slave of the lender," Proverbs 22:7), and many families have found freedom through that discipline. Then ask what they leave out. Proverbs 22:7 is taught to the borrower, but Scripture's usury law speaks to the lender. "Live like no one else, so later you can live like no one else" measures success as distance *above* the neighbor. And "financial peace" is measured on one household's balance sheet, where the neighbor never appears. Can a household be at peace while the gap between it and its neighbor keeps growing? Matthew Desmond's *Poverty, by America* answers with uncomfortable evidence: much of the comfort above the median rests on cheap labor, high rents and lending at the bottom. Peace with whom? (The aim is participation, not guilt: we are naming ourselves as actors inside systems of justice or injustice, never mere observers — the Polygons lesson, now personal.)

## Application — Three Lanes

- **Tech workers:** Practice redemption engineering: pick one algorithmic system in your reach and trace where it inherits bias — training data, proxies, edge-case handling — then design the correction, testing against the users most likely to be harmed first (Acts 10:34 as QA policy). Build real relationships with vulnerable users rather than personas of them. If you manage by dashboard, audit what your algorithmic management does to workers' agency and dignity.
- **Households:** Run the personal lifestyle audit: map how your consumption, housing, transportation, and giving actually land on your community's most vulnerable. Take up a 30-day "justice in practice" challenge of incremental changes — ethical purchasing, one advocacy act, one relationship across an economic boundary. Review your digital footprint by the same standard, and study ethical stewardship of family finances as discipleship, not decoration. Keep your real finances, gap included: find the median household income and wealth where you live and set your own beside them. Then ask what your distance from that median is *for*, and who it lets you help.
- **Church leaders:** Extend the audit to the whole institution — budget, facilities, hiring, worship accessibility across socioeconomic lines, and the church's digital presence. Launch a **Digital Jubilee initiative**: skills training, device access, and advocacy against digital exclusion in your parish footprint. Rethink missions and charity toward long-term development and root causes; teach Kingdom economics, beginning with a theology of giving that takes account of members' real finances: Deuteronomy's floor before Malachi's storehouse, a benevolence fund that pays out before the building fund, fair wages for the church's own staff and caregivers, and plain teaching on usury and debt; and keep the fruit dashboard honest about whom your community actually reaches. Where policy shapes injustice, be a prophetic witness in the public square — outcomes over lip service, as Module 7 committed.

## Conclusion — Movement IV Closes

The commons is rebuilt only when it is *full*. We have relearned truth-telling against the distortions, communal sensemaking as Kingdom Developers, and now justice as the boundary discipline: active inclusion until there is no more "still room." The German framers proved dignity can be made architectural; the banquet parable insists God will settle for nothing less. And the refrain has now sounded all three clauses in course order — walk humbly, love mercy, act justly — awaiting only its resolution at commissioning.

Movement V asks the final question: to what end? Justice fills the house — but what is the feast *for*? Jesus answered: "I have come that they may have life, and have it abundantly." Tomorrow we name the telos of everything we have built: **Module 13 — Helping People Thrive.**

## Go Deeper

This shelf is deliberately mixed, because the Polygons lesson applies to reading lists too: demand diversity near you, and expect to find here both a voice you trust and one that argues with you.

**Start here: Matthew Desmond, *Poverty, by America* (2023).** Desmond asks why the richest nation keeps so much poverty, and answers that the rest of us benefit from it: as employers paying low wages, as consumers of cheap goods and services, as landlords and lenders, and as households whose tax breaks dwarf what goes to the poor. It is this module's "real finances mind the gap" argued from the evidence, and it ends with a call to become what Desmond calls a *poverty abolitionist*: someone who refuses to profit from a neighbor's poverty. Read it with Deuteronomy 15 open beside it.
