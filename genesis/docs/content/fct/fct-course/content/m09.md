Module 8 centered love; this module builds its instrumentation. If we refuse to let engagement, attendance, and revenue define success, we still need something to watch — and Jesus supplied the discernment framework in agricultural form: by their fruit you will recognize them. This module closes Movement III by translating the fruits of the Spirit from devotional poster to working dashboard.

## Opening — Religion's True Metric

Read James 1:27: "Pure religion and undefiled before our God and Father is this: to visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction, and to keep oneself unspotted from the world." Scripture is not shy about metrics — it simply chooses shocking ones. Not size, not reach: orphans visited, widows attended, integrity kept. Pray: *Father, you measure what we have not thought to count. Teach us to watch what you watch, and to call nothing success that bears no fruit.*

## Humane Tech Concepts in View

This module pairs with FHT's *Centering Values, Part 2 — Implementing Value-Centered Approaches*:

- **Metric drift at scale.** Metrics make massive systems manageable, and optimizing them feels obviously good when users grow from 10 to 1,000. But at 1,000,000, the handful of numbers can no longer see the harms underneath: growth in GDP might mean more addicted citizens; growth in patients treated, more sick people; growth in engagement, more anxious teenagers. The metric outlives the value it was meant to serve.
- **Values inquiry as ongoing practice** — regularly re-asking what we value, integrating diverse stakeholders, discovering a system's *true* purpose by observing its behavior rather than reading its mission statement.
- **Trustworthy technology** has four components: integrity, competency, accountability, and education — alignment maintained even when the market rewards drift.
- **Practical tools:** retro-casting (working backward from the future we actually want) and value-sensitive design. And the pragmatic starting point: center metrics that reduce *known* harm — the low-hanging fruit, ready for harvest.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

1. Explain how metric optimization drifts from original values as systems scale, extending Goodhart's Law from Module 8 into lived examples.

3. Translate each fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22-23) into observable indicators for personal, product, and congregational life.
4. Analyze the four components of trustworthy technology as fruit-consistent institutional character.

6. Evaluate a real case of measuring known harm against institutional resistance, and articulate what faithfulness to the data required.

## Teaching

### Introduction: When the Number Outlives the Value

Quantification is a genuine gift — no one runs a system with a billion data points on vibes. A/B tests and dashboards summarize oceans of behavior into decisions that feel rigorous and objective. The trouble is subtle: metrics were installed to *serve* values, and at scale they quietly replace them. The bigger the system run on a handful of numbers, the easier it becomes to be out of touch with the harms underneath — and quantitative objectivity gets mistaken for ethical neutrality all the way down. The way out is not abandoning measurement but repenting of *what* we measure: choosing indicators that track the outcomes love actually cares about, starting with metrics that reduce known harm. The church sits inside this lesson, not outside it: Lifeway's 2026 discipleship study found the average churchgoer — thirty years attending, nearly five services a month — scoring a D+ across discipleship's marks. Decades of optimizing the countable did not grow the fruit.

### Scripture Engagement

**Matthew 7:15-20 — the anchor.** Jesus' warning about false prophets is an epistemology: "They come to you in sheep's clothing" — the branding is excellent, the stated values impeccable — "but by their fruit you will recognize them." Grapes don't come from thornbushes; a good tree *cannot* bear bad fruit, and a bad tree cannot bear good. Jesus authorizes outcome-based discernment: not the mission statement, not the intentions, not the production values — the fruit. Applied to technology: a platform whose fruit is anxiety, outrage, and isolation is a bad tree, whatever its About page says. Applied to church: the same test, unflinchingly.

**Galatians 5:22-23 — the dashboard.** "Love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control. Against such things there is no law." Nine observable qualities of persons and communities — qualitative, yes, but not unmeasurable. People can tell you whether they have peace; communities show whether they practice gentleness; self-control leaves visible traces in media habits and budgets. Paul's list is the counter-dashboard to the attention economy's: where engagement metrics track what technology extracts from people, the fruits track what the Spirit grows in them. And notice the reach of this test: the fruit hermeneutic by which Christians discern teachings — and even weigh texts — is the very one this course applies to technologies. One discernment, two domains.

**James 1:27 — the precedent.** Scripture has always been willing to name concrete indicators for spiritual realities. Orphans and widows visited is a *measure* — auditable, falsifiable, and entirely unlike counting rams and rivers of oil (Micah 6:7, yesterday's warning).

### Story: Measuring the Known Harm

Sheila Gregoire, a longtime advocate for healthy Christian marriages and a regular contributor to a major family ministry, grew troubled reading comment after comment from women describing how a best-selling marriage book had enabled abuse in their homes. She compiled the feedback into a meta-analysis and sent it to the ministry, asking them to respond to their own audience's testimony and stop promoting the book. They did not. So she did the work herself: a survey of more than 20,000 Christian women on marital and sexual satisfaction — real fruit inspection at scale. The findings showed that specific widely-taught beliefs measurably harmed the women taught them. She published the results in *The Great Sex Rescue*, proposing frameworks grounded in mutuality and respect. The institution's response was to remove her name from its website — even as newer contributors quietly adopted her most important conclusion without credit, still couched in frameworks her data had discredited.

Read this story with Matthew 7 open. Here was a teaching tree with impeccable sheep's clothing — bestselling, endorsed, institutionally protected — and one researcher who insisted on inspecting the fruit. Her method is this module's method: center the metrics that reduce known harm; trust outcomes over reputations; and expect that institutions optimizing for their own metrics may resist the harvest data. By their fruit — not their platform — you will recognize them.

### Eminence-Based vs. Evidence-Based Theology

The story has a shelf and a research arm. *The Great Sex Rescue* (2021, with Rebecca Gregoire Lindenbach and Joanna Sawatsky), *She Deserves Better* (2023), and *The Marriage You Want* (2025, with Keith Gregoire) each begin from the same wager: take Jesus at his word that a good tree cannot bear bad fruit — so survey tens of thousands, measure the outcomes the teachings actually produce, and follow the harvest data wherever it leads. And the work is now institutional: the **Good Fruit Faith Initiative** of the Bosko Foundation funds Bare Marriage's ongoing research and peer-reviewed publication — Matthew 7 with a methods section, and a standing invitation for the church to build its own evidence base.

Name what this replaces: **eminence-based theology** — teaching trusted because of who said it and how large the platform grew, the operating system of much of the last century's evangelical media. Jesus' test points the opposite direction: out of the citation circle of the eminent and into God's creation, where fruit can actually be observed. Taken seriously, "by their fruit you will recognize them" is a standing call to empirical research — and the scientific process itself (observe creation honestly, submit claims to testing, hold conclusions with epistemic humility, revise toward God's emergent, consensus truth) is a spiritual discipline by another name. It even offers a way past the church's political deadlocks: outcome data is neither red nor blue, and a people who covenant to inspect fruit together can set tribal priors aside long enough to see the light that creation is showing them.

This thought has a confessional address: the **Experience** arm of the Wesleyan Quadrilateral — Scripture, tradition, reason, experience, Albert Outler's map of John Wesley's method, with Outler's own insistence that Scripture stays primary (*prima scriptura*). Wesley called his theology *experimental divinity*, in the eighteenth-century sense of tested-in-experience, and he built the testing into church order: class and band meetings were structured weekly examination of souls' actual fruit — arguably the church's first longitudinal outcomes study — while Wesley himself ran electrical experiments and published home medicine. Empirical research is the Experience arm at scale, disciplined by Reason, under Scripture: not a rival authority but the quadrilateral doing its job. And the lineage runs wider than Methodism: the church has long read God's *two books*, Scripture and creation — "a most elegant book" (Belgic Confession, Article 2; Psalm 19:1-4; Romans 1:20) — so a church that inspects fruit empirically is simply reading the second book with care. To the charge that outcomes research is scientism invading theology, the reply is gentle: no — this is Wesley; this is the three-legged stool grown its fourth leg; this is the book of nature, open.

## Discussion & Activity

Central question: **If we truly believe that "by their fruit you will recognize them," how might we reimagine our measures of success and growth in the church to reflect the fruits of the Spirit?** Consider both personal spiritual development and communal church life. Then work the dashboard below in small groups: pick three fruits and draft one honest indicator each for your own congregation.

## A Fruit-of-the-Spirit Dashboard

Example measures a church might watch instead of attendance and budget:

| Fruit | What we might measure instead |
| --- | --- |
| Love | Meaningful relationships formed; reconciliations facilitated; sacrificial giving and service to those in need |
| Joy | Members' sense of purpose in their faith journey; the church's impact on community mental health; shared celebration |
| Peace | Conflicts resolved in church and community; reduction in domestic violence within the congregation's reach; local peacemaking involvement |
| Patience | Long-term discipleship commitment; retention and growth of new believers over years; endurance in slow community-development work |
| Kindness | Unexpected generosity; welcome and support of marginalized groups; care for creation |
| Goodness | Impact on local recovery and crime; ethical business practice among members; members standing against injustice |
| Faithfulness | Depth of Scripture engagement and application; sustained commitment to mission and service; integrity in personal and professional life |
| Gentleness | How disagreements are conducted; the tone of members' public and online speech; restorative approaches to correction |
| Self-control | Media consumption habits and their effects; responsible financial practice; stewardship of the church's own resources and footprint |

## Application — Three Lanes

- **Tech workers:** Draft fruit-KPIs for something you build — two indicators that would show your product growing peace, patience, or self-control in users, alongside one known-harm metric you commit to reducing. Audit your team against the four trustworthiness components (integrity, competency, accountability, education), then retro-cast: describe the fruit your product bears in five years if all goes *right*, and work backward to this quarter's decisions.
- **Households:** Institute a monthly family fruit check-in: each person names where they saw the fruits growing at home and where the household's systems (schedules, screens, spending) worked against them. Adjust one system, not one person.
- **Church leaders:** Begin the shift the dashboard implies: develop a fruit-of-the-Spirit assessment rhythm for individual and communal reflection; form small groups around cultivating specific fruits; pair mature believers with newer ones; redesign one program explicitly around the fruit it should bear; partner with local organizations who can tell you honestly what your community's fruit tastes like from outside. Let technology serve connection and accountability in this — never the scoreboard.

## Conclusion — Movement III Closes

Our loves are reordered and instrumented: love centered as the root dependency, and the fruits of the Spirit installed as the dashboard that keeps us honest about whether love is actually what we're growing. We carry two warnings forward — Micah's rivers of oil and Goodhart's Law, the same warning in two dialects — and one working method: inspect fruit, reduce known harm, trust outcomes over branding.

But fruit inspection has a prerequisite this movement has quietly assumed: the ability to *see clearly* — shared, trustworthy perception of what is actually happening. And that is precisely what our information environment now distorts at scale. More than half of U.S. counties have little or no local news; the feeds that replaced it are engineered for engagement, not truth. Movement IV rebuilds the commons of shared understanding, and it begins by learning to recognize the distortions: **Module 10 — Recognizing Distortions.**
