**Movement I — Waking Up · Day 1**

*Arc: from vague unease → to precisely naming the crisis generators, including the church's own attractional arms race.*

*Anchor text: 1 Samuel 8:10-22 · Supporting: Genesis 11:1-9; Matthew 23:3-4 with Luke 12:48; Proverbs 3:5-6 · Story: Expanding responsibility (Solomon, Josiah, James 3:1)*

Module 1 let us feel that something is wrong, and Module 2 gave us the lenses to see it; today we state it well — because a problem well stated is half solved. This is the diagnosis session: the metacrisis named, its generators identified, and the church located honestly inside it. A line from the systems philosopher Daniel Schmachtenberger frames the day: *we can't have the power of gods without the wisdom, love, and prudence of gods.*

## Opening — Confessing Our Limits

Diagnosis begins with epistemic humility. Read Proverbs 3:5-6 together — "trust in the LORD with all your heart and lean not on your own understanding" — and pray for clear sight: *Lord, before we name what is wrong with our systems, guard us from the pride that built them — and equally from the false humility that keeps quiet before thrones. We lean not on our own understanding, and we bow to no understanding lesser than yours. Make us creatures with clear eyes, not gods — and give us Samuel's nerve: to read the costs aloud.*

## Humane Tech Concepts in View

This module receives the full diagnostic taxonomy (moved here from Day 0, where we only needed to feel it):

- **The metacrisis / polycrisis** — the interconnected web of global challenges: god-like technology, truth decay, the attention economy, institutional degradation, social isolation, and the breaking of our epistemic commons. Not six problems; one system.
- **Schmachtenberger's three generators of existential risk** — exponential technology outpacing our capacity to guide it; tragedy of the commons at global scale; inadequate collective sense-making and choice-making.
- **Prioritized responses** — design systems that keep participants maximally informed; couple responsibility to rights, privileges, and power as they grow; build a fourth estate (a media culture) that can truthfully inform amid exponential information systems.

The move this module makes: each generator has a church expression. Rivalrous dynamics → the worship-experience arms race. Tragedy of the commons → congregational externalities. Broken sense-making → a church that reads one Bible and lives a hundred contradictory gospels.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

1. Define the metacrisis and name Schmachtenberger's three generators of existential risk.
2. Translate each generator into its church expression: attractional arms races, congregational externalities, and an eroded epistemic commons.
3. Interpret 1 Samuel 8 as the pattern of trading agency for god-like power, and evaluate where church and society repeat Israel's demand.
4. Articulate the biblical coupling of responsibility with power (Luke 12:48; Matthew 23:3-4; James 3:1) as the standard exponential capability must meet.
5. Propose one "third way" design principle for their own community — beyond authoritarian control and liberal chaos.

## Teaching

### Introduction: Stating the Problem Well

How do we increase the epistemic capacity of the church — how the church knows what it knows — until our common life in Christ is cohesive enough to handle controversial topics without breakdown? To diagnose the church's challenge correctly, we must learn to discern the generators of risk intuitively, in our own house first:

- **Arms races.** What do the church's arms races look like? Worship experiences, programs, production values. We are competing for dwindling attention in a hyper-individualized world — workism, a cost-of-living crisis, a care crisis — that leaves less rest and fewer resources for any community, the church included. The empirical grade is now in: Lifeway's 2026 State of Discipleship study scores the average U.S. Protestant churchgoer 68 out of 100 — a D+ after an average thirty years of attendance at nearly five services a month — with Sharing Christ the weakest of all eight marks. The attractional era optimized attendance and did not produce disciples.
- **Tragedy of the commons.** What externalities does our church community generate? Consider the provocation of "Joe Christian," who gives righteously to missions serving immigrants, orphans, and Christian education — and votes in ways that offload the costs of those same commitments onto the commons. Toxic expressions of charity, mission, and tax-aversion are externalities too.
- **Subsuming the substrate.** Complicated systems consuming the complex substrate that sustains them grow fragile until collapse is imminent. Where are we eroding trust, watering down sound theology to stay seeker-friendly, sheltering systemic injustice, letting operating costs outrun our capacity? At what point do *we* become Mars Hill — so superficial, so leveraged, so complicated that one crisis ends us?
- **Exponential technology.** In weapons: knife → gun → atomic bomb → hydrogen bomb. In formation: a once-a-week sermon against 24/7, multi-channel, personally curated feeds — and now AI deep fakes and a potential emergent cult of superintelligence in a post-truth world full of truth weapons. If we gain the power of gods without the love and wisdom of God, we self-destruct.

### Scripture Engagement

**1 Samuel 8:10-22 — the anchor.** Israel demands a king "such as all the other nations have." Samuel states the problem well — itemizing exactly what the king will *take*: your sons, your daughters, your fields, a tenth of your grain and flocks; "and you yourselves will become his slaves... and you will cry out in that day." The people hear the terms of service read aloud and accept them anyway. This is the ancient shape of our present dilemma: god-like power was not imposed on us — we asked for it, to be like the nations, and the warning label changed nothing. Notice what God grieves: "it is not you they have rejected, but they have rejected me as their king." A misplaced desire for power is, underneath, a displacement of trust.

**Genesis 11:1-9 — Babel.** "Nothing they propose to do will now be impossible for them" — exponential capability in service of self-elevation, one language, one tower, one feed. God's mercy comes as decentralization. Babel warns us that a unified information system is not the same thing as a healthy epistemic commons.

**Matthew 23:3-4 and Luke 12:48 — responsibility coupled to power.** The Pharisees tie heavy loads on other shoulders and lift nothing themselves — religious leadership generating externalities. Against this: "to whom much was given, much will be required." This is the biblical design principle the humane tech movement rediscovered: responsibilities must scale with rights, privileges, and power.

### Story: Expanding Responsibility, Not Empire

Scripture's counter-pattern to 1 Samuel 8: God's people were to grow influence through faithfulness, not through treaties and chariots. Solomon's early reign rests on wisdom and justice rather than military expansion; Josiah's reforms return the people to God's law rather than extend the kingdom's borders. The New Testament sharpens it: Jesus inverts power into servanthood, and James 3:1 warns that teachers will be judged more strictly — greater platform, stricter judgment. The biblical trajectory is expanding responsibility, never expanding extraction.

## Discussion & Activity

1. **Name our arms races.** In groups, list concrete places your congregation competes for attention. Which would Samuel read aloud as "what the king will take"?
2. **The mirror questions.** What are you asking of "church" and the leaders who run this institution? What expectations are you setting for *yourself* as part of the Body of Christ? (These two questions expose whether we relate to church as consumers or as members.)
3. **Externalities inventory.** Return to the Joe Christian provocation. Where do our charity and our politics offload costs onto the very people we say we serve? Keep this list — Module 6 gives it a formal framework.

## Application — Three Lanes

- **Tech workers:** Take the three generators to work. Which one is your product or org most exposed to — an arms race, an externality, or broken sense-making? Write the one-sentence problem statement for it; a problem well stated is half solved.
- **Households:** Name your household's arms races — activities, achievements, appearances kept up "to be like the nations." Choose one to state plainly at the dinner table this week.
- **Church leaders:** Success cannot be defined by attention, production value, or programs. Audit where the attractional model — church growth by staging ever-more-compelling experiences — has metastasized in your context, and note where Sunday's message is filtered by audience capture. Consider counterweights that restore honest speech — such as balancing career preaching with visiting ministers who are independent enough to say hard, existentially important truths.

## Conclusion

If increasing our capacity for coordination is the secular imperative of the metacrisis, the Christian frame asks more: what is the church's role in reconciling Christ in the world? Between the two broad attractors — authoritarian oppression and liberal chaos — the narrow path is a third way (Matthew 7:13-14): systems that keep participants maximally informed, that couple responsibility to power as it grows, and a truth-telling media culture the church helps embody rather than outsource. We already know what that community looks like when it appears: a people so reconciled they hold all things in common and need no king but Christ (Acts 2:42-47) — Jesus' own prayer "that they may be one" (John 17) taken seriously in the face of every reason to divide.

But stating the problem reveals the real deficit. It was never information; Israel had the information — Samuel read it aloud. The deficit is wisdom, and the trust that lets wisdom travel between us. That is tomorrow's work: **Module 4 — The Wisdom Gap & the Conditions for Trust.**
