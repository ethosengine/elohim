**Movement II — Turning · Day 3**

*Arc: from attention as commodity → to attention as the dwelling place God desires.*

*Anchor text: Luke 10:38-42 · Supporting: Deuteronomy 11:18-21; Revelation 3:20; Colossians 3:1-3 with Hebrews 3:1 · Story: A God who wants to dwell with his people (Exodus to Emmanuel)*

Movement II begins here, and it begins with repentance's first territory: attention. Movement I taught us to see and to name; now we turn — and the turning starts where the attention economy attacks first, because the ground it monetizes is the very ground on which God has always sought to meet us. The industry calls it engagement inventory. Scripture calls it the heart's dwelling place.

## Opening — The One Who Knocks

Read Revelation 3:20: "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears my voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and eat with him, and he with me." Notice the manners of Christ: he knocks. He does not autoplay, does not push through, does not let himself in. The Lord of all things waits for attention freely given — and everything competing for us today does not. Pray: *Lord, you stand at the door and knock, and we have filled the house with noise. Quiet us until we can hear you — and give us the will to open.*

## Humane Tech Concepts in View

This module pairs with FHT's *Respecting Human Nature*:

- **Heuristics become biases.** We inherit fast decision-making shortcuts evolved to keep our ancestors alive. In modern contexts those heuristics misfire as cognitive biases — and biases can be systematically exploited. Every human brain is subject to these forces, so technology that targets them affects everyone.
- **The exploitation problem.** "Giving people what they want" collapses when what people *click* diverges from what people *value*. Continuous A/B testing runs uncontrolled psychological experiments on billions, optimizing for the exploit, not the person.
- **The earlier threshold.** We fear the moment AI overwhelms human strengths — but there was an earlier moment, already behind us, when it overwhelmed human *weaknesses*.
- **The humane alternative.** Treat human vulnerabilities as design constraints rather than profit centers: build for users' values and goals, not engagement; enable wise choices; use tools like the Humane Tech Design Guide to assess what we build.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

1. Explain how evolved heuristics become exploitable biases, and why exploitation at scale implicates every user and builder.
2. Critique "giving people what they want" and mass A/B testing as an ethics of exploitation dressed as service.
3. Trace the biblical arc — Sinai's dwelling, the Shema's bound words, Emmanuel, the knock at the door — as the story of a God who seeks freely given attention as the site of communion.
4. Apply the Mary and Martha story to personal and congregational attention practices, distinguishing the necessary from the one thing needed.
5. Map one product or experience with the Humane Tech Design Guide, redesigning it to respect attention.
6. Structure community life within real human relational capacity (the Dunbar layers) as an act of respecting human nature.

## Teaching

### Introduction: The Ground Being Fought Over

The attention economy and the living God are, in a precise sense, after the same thing — and only one of them asks. Our brains make fast decisions on incomplete data; the shortcuts that once kept us alive are now catalogued, tested, and triggered at industrial scale. The result is a battle for the heart's doorway fought with tools the heart did not evolve to resist. Humane technology answers by taking our vulnerabilities as constraints to honor rather than surfaces to attack. Scripture goes further: it tells us *why* attention deserves that honor. Attention is not merely a cognitive resource. It is where love begins, where formation happens, where God has always chosen to meet his people.

### Scripture Engagement

**Luke 10:38-42 — the anchor.** Martha is not doing anything wrong — she is doing many *necessary* things, and that is exactly the trap: "distracted by all the preparations," worried and upset about many things. Mary sits at Jesus' feet, and Jesus names the economy of the moment: "few things are needed — or indeed only one. Mary has chosen what is better, and it will not be taken away from her." Attention given to Christ is the one investment nothing can claw back. Our feeds are Martha's kitchen at planetary scale — endless legitimate-seeming preparations engineered to keep us from sitting down. The question this module asks of every technology and every church program: does it help people choose what is better, or does it multiply preparations?

**Deuteronomy 11:18-21 — the callback.** "Lay up these words of mine in your heart and in your soul, and you shall bind them as a sign on your hand, and they shall be as frontlets between your eyes." Module 1 introduced the tefillin as Israel's attention technology; here Moses extends the design brief to the whole environment: speak of them sitting at home, walking on the road, lying down, rising up; write them on doorframes and gates. God commands an *ambient* attention architecture — the words woven into hands, eyes, thresholds, and transitions. Our devices have taken the hand, the eyes, the doorway, and the first and last moments of the day. The Shema's design pattern is waiting to be reclaimed.

**Revelation 3:20 with Hebrews 3:1 and Colossians 3:1-3 — consent and direction.** Christ knocks and waits — divine respect for human attention. "Consider Jesus" (Hebrews 3:1) — fix your attention deliberately. "Set your minds on things above" (Colossians 3:1-3) — and then Paul immediately embodies it: put on compassion, kindness, humility, forgiveness. Directed attention is not escapism; it is the upstream act that determines what we put on.

### Story: A God Who Wants to Dwell

The Exodus is the story of a God who wants his people's attention — not as surveillance, but as communion. He delivers them *in order to dwell among them*: "let them make me a sanctuary, that I may dwell in their midst" (Exodus 25:8). The tabernacle sits at the literal center of the camp; the words are bound to hands and foreheads; the feasts structure the year — an entire civilization designed around shared attention to God. The arc completes in Jesus: Emmanuel, God with us, the Word who "became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14) — and presses further still: God dwelling not only *with* us but *in* our hearts and minds. Revelation 3:20 is that whole story compressed to a single image: the God of Sinai, reduced by love to knocking at a door we are too distracted to hear. The attention economy is not merely stealing our time. It is squatting in the sanctuary.

## Discussion & Activity

1. **Martha audit.** Where is our worship service, our programming, our family calendar "worried and upset about many things"? Which preparations exist to serve attention to Christ, and which have quietly replaced it?
2. **The manners of Christ.** Christ knocks; our apps let themselves in. List the technologies in your life by their manners: which ones wait to be invited, and which barge through? What would church communication look like if it kept Christ's manners?
3. **Activity — Design Guide mapping.** Using the Humane Tech Design Guide, map an experience you seek to create with technology (no product of your own? pick any existing one). Identify which human vulnerabilities it currently touches, then redesign one flow so the vulnerability becomes a design constraint — a sacred pause, a natural stopping point, a default that honors the user's stated values.

## Application — Three Lanes

- **Tech workers:** Build attention sanctuaries — spaces in your product that facilitate depth rather than churn. Add cognitive-bias safeguards that help users notice their own reactivity; integrate value-alignment moments ("is this how you meant to spend this hour?"); implement sacred pauses in flows that currently exploit momentum. Bring your Design Guide map to Module 8 with your Module 4 wisdom-feature sketch.
- **Households:** Module 1's audit now becomes covenant. Reclaim the Shema's architecture for your home: guard the thresholds (first and last thirty minutes of the day device-free), the table (a screen-free meal as daily liturgy), and the doorframes (chargers live outside bedrooms). Filter what enters by Philippians 4:8 — whatever is true, noble, right, pure, lovely — as the household's content policy.
- **Church leaders:** Structure community within real human capacity. The Dunbar layers — anthropologist Robin Dunbar's nested limits of human relationship: roughly 150 acquaintances, 50 casual friends, 15 close, 5 intimate — are the created limits of relational attention; design your congregation's groups, care structures, and expectations to honor them rather than pretending one pastor or one feed can hold everyone. And protect the sacred hour: audit Sunday itself for engineered distraction — countdown clocks, announcements, production — asking of each element the Mary question: does this help people sit down at Jesus' feet?

## Conclusion

We will never be unencumbered by our biases; that is part of being human, and God has known it all along — he designed a whole civilization of bound words, doorframe inscriptions, and festival rhythms to work *with* human nature toward communion. The attention economy proves the same anthropology in reverse: it works with human nature toward extraction. Both can't hold the ground. Attention is a divine gift, part of what it means to bear God's image: the capacity to consider, to behold, to sit at someone's feet. Technology worthy of humans protects that vulnerability, complements our sense-making, and supports the discourse by which we choose well together.

But here is Movement II's next turn: captured attention is not only a private loss. What captures us costs people we never see — the moderator, the mined, the neighbor left on the road while we scroll past. Tomorrow we learn to count those costs: **Module 6 — Externalities & the Commons.**
