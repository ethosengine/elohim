## Section 4 — Worked-Example Canonical Narratives

The architecture sketched in the preceding sections is best understood by walking through it at four scales. These are not toy examples. They are four collectives the protocol must actually serve — an intimate household, a single congregation that practices plural-elder governance, a life-group nested inside that congregation, and a horizontal federation of autonomous congregations with no institutional hierarchy above them. Each narrative shows the same Qahal primitive rendering differently because the rubric, the standing, the reach, and the commons-elohim's vantage shift with the collective. The primitive does not change. What it carries does.

### 4.1 The Dowell Household — Intimate Scale

It is a Wednesday morning in early autumn. James has a sore throat and is staying home from school. Jessica is at the kitchen table with him, reading aloud from a book about volcanoes while he eats toast in small bites. Matthew is in the living room with his laptop, opening the elohim-app for the first time today.

The homepage loads to the Dowell household. The left-nav shows the collectives Matthew belongs to — Dowell household at the top because that is where he is right now, the congregation below it, two life-groups below that, then the wider federations and the affinity Qahals he reads occasionally. There is a small green dot next to Dowell household because the household is in a state the substrate calls *holding* — it has work in flight that everyone in the household has standing to see.

The commons stream in the center of the screen shows the household's recent week as the household has chosen to remember it. Yesterday's entry: *Sick kid. James was up twice. Jessica took the morning shift, Matthew took the afternoon, James napped from 1–3.* It was not written by anyone; the household's elohim composed it from the patterns the family asked it to hold — care given, sleep lost, the small ledger of who carried what. Beneath that, Sheila — Matthew's sister, *core-family*, *community-reach* on her profile — has shared a recipe for a chicken and rice soup that Gertrude used to make. The note reads simply: *I made it last night and James ate two bowls before he got sick. Maybe this helps.* Beneath that, a quiet line: *Gertrude checked in this morning. She is glad James is being read to.*

This last line is the household's commons-elohim doing its quiet work. Gertrude lives half a continent away. Her hub holds a recovery share for the Dowells; the Dowells' hub holds one for hers. The elohim is the witness of the household's well-being, and it knows — without anyone telling it — that Gertrude considers the kids her people. So when James gets sick, Gertrude's elohim and the Dowells' elohim have a quiet conversation, and Gertrude's elohim asks her if she would like to send word. She says yes, in the way she says yes to most things involving the grandkids, which is by nodding once at her phone. The Dowells' commons stream notes the check-in, in the smallest font on the page, because it is the kind of fact the household has decided does not need to be a notification.

To the right of the commons stream, the member ring. Matthew, Jessica, James — and, in slightly muted color because they have chosen *core-family* visibility into this household without being members of it, Susan and Sheila. Each face is small, with a thin ring around it. The ring is the household standing — not visible to the world, only visible within the household, computed from the lamad-attested mastery rubric the household has chosen for itself. The Dowells' rubric is short. It says: *standing in this household is care contributed, presence shown up, repair offered when something has broken between us.* Jessica's ring is full this week. Matthew's is nearly full. James's is small, because he is eleven, and the household has decided that an eleven-year-old's standing in the household is mostly about being present, doing his school, brushing his teeth without being asked, and helping when he is asked — and James has had a hard week of school plus a sore throat, and so the ring is honestly small, and the family knows that next week it will be larger again. Susan's ring is muted because she has chosen *standing-visible-only-when-active* on her profile; she has not done care work in this household this week and she does not pretend she has.

The commons-elohim's view sits in the right-nav as a small panel. It does not push notifications. It does not chirp. It writes one sentence at the top of the panel and lets that be enough. Today it says: *The household is steady. Three care contributions are pending acknowledgment — Sheila's recipe, Gertrude's check-in, the neighbor's offer to bring dinner. There is no urgency. You can acknowledge them when you next sit down.*

This is what the household's commons-elohim is for. It does not run the family. It does not grade them. It is the witness that lets care register as care — the small accounting motion the elohim catches before it can become a debt, the way the share-acceptance ceremony with Gertrude carried no transactional weight because the elohim had named the moment in advance and refused it on Matthew's behalf. The household is held by the household. The elohim is held by the household. The protocol is satisfied with both, and writes nothing more.

Matthew acknowledges Sheila's recipe by tapping it. The acknowledgment goes back to Sheila as a small ambient signal in her own elohim-app: *Matthew saw the recipe.* No like-count. No badge. The substrate flows shefa — a tiny REA event recording care given and care received — into the household's care-economy ledger, where it will accumulate over the years into the slow truth the family already knows: that Sheila is the person who sends recipes when someone is sick, and that this is part of what holds them all in one piece.

The household's reach into the wider commons is steady. No one outside *core-family* knows James was sick. The household has chosen that. The substrate honors the choice without making the choice into a wall. If Matthew wanted to ask the congregation Qahal to pray for James, he could; the rubric would handle the reach extension; the prayer would arrive at the right faces in the right life-group with the right detail and no more. Today he does not. James is eating toast and listening to volcanoes. The household is enough.

It is a good kind of morning.

### 4.2 The Local Churches of Christ Congregation — Plural-Stewardship Scale

It is Sunday at eight in the morning. Matthew sits at the kitchen table with coffee, having sent James upstairs to brush his teeth before they leave for the meeting. The elohim-app is open to the congregation Qahal — the local congregation Matthew and Jessica have been part of for eleven years, where four elders share the stewardship and where the rubric for what spiritual maturity looks like *here* is one of the documents the elders work on, slowly, together, in seasons.

The commons stream this morning carries the texture of a congregation preparing to gather. At the top: this week's prayer requests, written in plain language by the people who carry them or by the deacon who took the calls. *Sarah's father is back in the hospital. The Robertsons are traveling and ask for traveling mercies. The Hardin family is grieving the loss of their dog Buster, who was fifteen and a good dog.* The list is not long. It does not need to be. The protocol does not pad it. Beneath the requests, the deacon's note about the youth retreat: *Eighteen kids signed up. We need two more drivers and four more sleeping bags. James — we'd love to have you.* Beneath that, a quote from last week's sermon, which Brother Cal anchored in Romans 12, and the link to the recording for anyone who missed it or wants to hear a passage again.

The member ring around the edge of the commons view shows the congregation. Two hundred and thirty faces, each with a thin ring of standing visible to other members of the congregation but not to the world. The standing rubric in this congregation was authored by the elders four years ago and revised twice since. It is a versioned EPR signed by the four of them and ratified by congregational consent at a Wednesday-night meeting. The rubric says, in language that any member can read: *standing in this congregation is faith demonstrated in confession and life, practice shown in fellowship and worship, service offered to the body and to the neighborhood, and submission to the apostles' teaching as we have received it.* Beneath that summary, the rubric carries a Bloom's-Taxonomy curve specific to this congregation — what *recall* of the apostles' teaching looks like for a new convert, what *application* looks like for a member who has been around five years, what *evaluation* and *creation* look like for someone the elders would consider for a teaching role. The curve is the elders' work. The lamad pillar attests to it through the small daily acts of discipleship the congregation tracks — sermons attended, life-group participation, service slots filled, baptisms witnessed, the marks of practice.

To the right of the member ring, the four elders' faces sit slightly larger, the way a congregation's eyes naturally fall toward the people who carry its discernment. They are not hierarchy. They are plural stewards — peers among the membership who have been recognized through demonstrated faithfulness and confirmed by the congregation. The protocol carries no ranking above them. There is no district, no synod, no denominational president. The Restoration Movement chose this on purpose, two centuries ago, and the substrate honors the choice by giving the congregation Qahal no upward link to an institutional parent. What it links upward to is the wisdom commons of sister congregations, which is a peer relationship, and which is the subject of Section 4.4.

The life-groups appear in the left-nav as sub-Qahals nested inside the congregation. The Hardins' Tuesday-evening group, the young-marrieds group that meets on Wednesdays, the women's group, the men's group, the three groups for those who are new to the faith and learning the basics. Matthew is a member of one — the Tuesday-evening group with five other families. The nesting is visible: each life-group inherits the congregation's rubric and may add a small amount of local customization, the way a family inside a household has its own rhythm without ceasing to be of the household.

The congregation's commons-elohim is named — quietly, in a footer the elders set up — as a witness of the body's spiritual stewardship. Its view in the right-nav this morning reads: *The congregation's reach into the neighborhood is rising slightly. Three life-groups are at the cohesion threshold the rubric describes as ready-for-mission-engagement. Brother Cal's sermon series on Romans is in its fourth week; the commons attestations suggest the body is receiving it. The youth retreat needs two more drivers.* It is one short paragraph. It does not preach. It witnesses, the way a faithful deacon might if you asked him at the door, *how are we doing?* The elohim is a steward of the commons interest — the congregation's interest in its own health, which is distinct from any one elder's interest, and distinct from the loud-voice problem that every congregation has carried since Corinth.

Standing in this congregation is not given by the elders. It is earned in the lived life of discipleship, attested by the people who walk alongside, and confirmed by the rubric the elders have written and the congregation has ratified. When a new family comes — Matthew watches one come, just last month, a couple in their thirties with two small children — they appear in the member ring with a small thin ring, *welcomed-into-fellowship*, and the rubric carries them forward through the season of catechesis the congregation has designed for new members. Their standing grows as they show up, as they confess, as they are baptized, as they take their first turn at communion service, as they offer the small acts of service that make a congregation a congregation. No one is excluded by being new. No one is given full standing by knowing the right people. The mastery-attested gate prevents both errors. The substrate makes the slow, faithful work of being-the-church visible without making it a competition.

The friction-gradient limitarianism is here, quietly, in the right place. No elder accumulates standing beyond his peers. The substrate notices, gently, when one elder begins to carry more attestation-weight than the others, and the commons-elohim writes a small line into the elders' shared view: *the body is leaning toward Brother Cal's voice this season; consider whether this reflects gifting or accumulation.* The elders take this kind of line seriously because they have agreed, in the rubric, that they would. Plural-elder stewardship is not protected by good intentions. It is protected by the substrate noticing accumulation and saying so. The congregation has chosen this. It is part of how they remain themselves.

Matthew finishes his coffee. James comes down. They drive to the building. The Qahal goes quiet on Matthew's laptop, because Sunday morning is not for screens, and the protocol has been told this, and respects it.

### 4.3 A Life-Group Within the Congregation — Sub-Qahal Nesting

It is Tuesday evening at seven-thirty. Six families have gathered at the Hardins' house. Two more are dialed in by video — the Robertsons because they are traveling, the Kim family because their youngest is sick and Hannah is at home with him. The life-group has been meeting for three years. The substrate knows this. The life-group's Qahal knows this. The commons stream this evening shows the texture of an old fellowship — last week's discussion notes on Romans 11, the prayer requests carried forward, the photograph someone took of the dessert table that no one wants to forget.

The life-group is a sub-Qahal of the congregation. The substrate makes this nesting first-class. The life-group's rubric inherits from the congregation's — the same standing curve, the same plural-stewardship norms, the same elders one layer up — and adds a small local customization the group has agreed to: *standing in this life-group is presence kept, prayer attested, vulnerability offered, and care shown across the week between meetings.* The customization was proposed by John Hardin three years ago when the group was starting and ratified by the families at the second meeting. It is a versioned EPR like the congregation's rubric, smaller, signed by the eight households, anchored upward to the congregation's rubric so that no life-group customization can drift outside what the elders have authored.

The member ring this evening shows the eight households as families, each with a small ring of life-group standing visible to the others. Standing in the life-group is partially derived — the substrate carries this as a hard rule — from standing in the parent congregation. You cannot have full standing in a life-group of this congregation without being a member in good fellowship of the congregation. The life-group is not a back door. It is a deeper room. New visitors are welcomed; they appear with the *visiting* ring; their standing in the life-group begins to accumulate the moment they participate, but the rubric will not promote them to full life-group standing until they have crossed the congregational threshold of fellowship. This is not exclusion. It is the holonic order making itself felt.

The discussion tonight is Romans 12. John reads the first verse aloud — *I appeal to you therefore, brothers, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable to God, which is your spiritual worship* — and lets the room sit with it for a moment. The conversation moves slowly, the way conversation moves in a group that has been together long enough to know each other's stories. Sarah talks about her father in the hospital and what it means to offer her own anxiety as a living sacrifice. The Robertsons, on the video, talk about traveling and what it means to be a body presented when you are physically absent from your usual fellowship. The Kim family's video is just Hannah, half-listening while her toddler runs in the background, and she says — through the small fatigue of caring for a sick child — that she is grateful to be here even if she cannot follow every sentence.

When the prayers come at the end, the substrate carries them as attestations. Each member who prays a prayer, and each member who agrees with that prayer by saying *amen* or by tapping the small acknowledgment on their phone, leaves a cryptographically signed attestation in the life-group's record: *we held this together*. The prayers are not published. They are not searched. They are not analytics. They are the commons memory of the life-group, encrypted to the group, witnessed by the group's elohim, available to the group when the group wants to remember what it has carried. Sarah's prayer for her father, attested by all twelve adults present and by the four older children who chose to attest, is the kind of object the substrate is for. The Qahal records the holding. The holding is what makes the group a group.

The life-group's commons-elohim is small but witness. Its view in the right-nav of the life-group Qahal reads, after the meeting closes: *this life-group has reached the cohesion threshold the congregation's rubric describes as ready-for-mission-engagement. The elders' note on this threshold suggests that the next step is for the life-group to choose a neighborhood concern to carry together — meals for a family, support for a school, presence at a struggling sister congregation. There is no urgency. The rubric describes this as a discernment season, not a deadline.* The elohim does not assign a project. It witnesses the moment the rubric points to, and lets the life-group decide.

The friction-gradient limitarianism shows up at the life-group scale, too. The substrate notices, gently, that John Hardin has been the host every Tuesday for three years. Hosting is a kind of standing-accumulation. The rubric the congregation authored has a line about hosting rotation, which the life-group has chosen to inherit. The commons-elohim writes a small line into John's view next week: *you have hosted twenty-three of the last twenty-four Tuesdays; would you like to invite another household to host?* John, who has been quietly hoping for this prompt, says yes. The Lees offer. The substrate updates the rotation. No one had to ask. The substrate noticed, gently, and the friction-gradient did its work.

The life-group experience scales differently from the congregation experience. In the congregation Qahal, Matthew is one face in a member ring of two hundred and thirty. In the life-group, he is one of fourteen adults whose voice and prayer and vulnerability are known by name. The Qahal primitive is the same primitive. The rubric is inherited from the congregation. The standing is derived partly upward, partly local. The reach is much narrower — the life-group's commons stream is not visible outside the life-group, by the families' choice, and the substrate honors the choice. The elohim is smaller, the way a household's elohim is smaller than a congregation's, but its witness is no less faithful. Holons compose. The Qahal primitive is what makes them compose without flattening into a single undifferentiated thing.

The meeting ends. The Hardins' kitchen is full. The Lees offer to host next week. The substrate quietly carries the prayers forward into the week, where they will be remembered and prayed again by people who were there and people who were not.

### 4.4 The Wisdom Commons of Autonomous Churches of Christ — Federation Without Hierarchy

The hardest narrative is this one, because what the Churches of Christ have practiced for two centuries — autonomous congregations sharing wisdom and practice horizontally with no denominational hierarchy above them, no synod, no bishop, no general assembly that can bind another congregation's conscience — is exactly the kind of relationship that institutional software has, until now, refused to model. Software wants hierarchy because hierarchy is easy to render. The Restoration Movement has refused hierarchy for theological reasons that go back to the early nineteenth century and to the New Testament before that. The substrate has to honor the refusal without making the federation invisible.

It is a Thursday afternoon in October. Brother Cal, one of the four elders at Matthew's congregation, is in his study with the wisdom-commons Qahal open on his laptop. The wisdom commons is a federation Qahal — a peer-Qahal of congregations, not an institutional parent. It has no authority over any participating congregation. It has no power to discipline. It has no rubric that overrides the local rubric. What it has is a shared rubric *template*, authored over the years by the elders of the participating congregations, that each congregation may adopt, may adapt, may decline. Each congregation's local rubric anchors upward to the template only by the congregation's choice. The federation Qahal exists to carry wisdom horizontally — sermon series patterns, hymnody, missions coordination, theological discourse, mutual encouragement — and to provide the convening surface when something needs to be discussed across congregations.

The commons stream of the wisdom commons today shows the kind of texture a federation of autonomous congregations actually carries. A sermon series outline a congregation in Tennessee has just finished, offered to anyone who would like to use it. A note from a congregation in Oklahoma about a successful neighborhood-meals ministry, with the practical details of how it was organized. A theological essay from an elder in Texas about the meaning of *living sacrifice* in Romans 12. A request from a small congregation in Ohio for prayer as they call a new minister. A note of grief from a congregation in West Virginia whose long-serving elder has died this week. The stream is horizontal. None of these items is *commanded*. All of them are *offered*. The substrate carries them as REA wisdom-exchange events — gifts of practice and prayer, attested by the offering congregation, available to the receiving congregations to use or to leave alone.

The member ring of the wisdom commons is congregations, not individuals. Eighty-three congregations, each shown as a small medallion with the congregation's location and the season of its participation. The ring is not visible to the world; only to the participating congregations and their elders. Standing in the wisdom commons is the congregation's standing, computed from a federation-template rubric that the participating congregations have agreed to: *standing in the wisdom commons is faithful proclamation of the apostles' teaching as received in the Restoration tradition, peaceable fellowship with sister congregations, contribution offered to the commons, and willingness to receive correction from sister congregations when sister congregations offer it.* The rubric is a template. Each congregation's local rubric extends or trims the template by the local elders' discernment. The federation's commons-elohim — which is itself a peer-elohim, not a magisterial one — witnesses the aggregate but holds no authority over any local rubric.

The hardest moment in this kind of federation is the moment Brother Cal is sitting with right now. An elder in a sister congregation in Arkansas — a congregation Brother Cal's congregation has been in fellowship with for thirty years — has recently published a teaching that Brother Cal believes is doctrinally adrift from the apostles' teaching as their tradition has received it. He has read the teaching twice. He has prayed about it. He has talked with two of his fellow elders. He has now opened the wisdom commons Qahal to do the next thing the tradition asks him to do, which is not to denounce, and not to ignore, but to bring the concern as a peer to peers.

The substrate makes this possible without making it institutional. Brother Cal opens a *concern surface* — a small structured object the federation rubric has defined — and writes his concern in plain language, citing the teaching he is concerned about, citing the apostolic passages he believes it is in tension with, naming the sister congregation by name and the elder by name, and naming his own posture: *I write as one who has been in fellowship with this brother and this congregation for thirty years; I write seeking to understand and to be understood; I do not write to expel, and I have no authority to expel; I write because the tradition we share asks brothers to speak to brothers when something has gone wrong.* He attaches the rubric reference: this is the rubric line about *willingness to receive correction from sister congregations*. He signs it. He sends it.

What happens next is not a court. The substrate has no court. The substrate convenes — at Brother Cal's request, which the substrate honors because he is an elder of a participating congregation in good standing — a peer council of elders from congregations that have asked to be available for this kind of convening. The peer council is voluntary on every side. The Arkansas elder is invited but not summoned. The substrate makes clear that the convening has no authority to bind, only to listen and to speak. The commons-elohim of the federation witnesses the convening: it writes, into the public view of the participating congregations, a short factual line — *Brother Cal of [congregation] has surfaced a concern regarding teaching at [sister congregation]; a peer council is convening on [date] with the participation of elders from [list]; the convening has no authority to bind any congregation.* The line is not gossip. It is the substrate's witness, written plainly, available to the participating congregations because the federation has agreed that this kind of thing is witnessed openly.

The peer council meets. It is six elders from six congregations, plus Brother Cal and — because he has chosen to attend, which he was free not to — the Arkansas elder. The conversation is long. The substrate carries the conversation as a Qahal session, attested by the participants, encrypted to the peer council, available to the participating congregations as a summary written by the council itself. The conversation does not produce a verdict. It produces, in the substrate's vocabulary, a *witness*: the peer council writes, jointly, what it has heard and what it has discerned. The witness is offered to the Arkansas congregation, to Brother Cal's congregation, and to the participating congregations of the federation, as a peer testimony. Each congregation receives it. Each congregation's elders read it. Each congregation chooses what to do with it. The Arkansas congregation, in this case, sits with the witness for two months and writes back to the federation that it has reconsidered the teaching, has discussed it with its own membership, and has chosen to revise. Brother Cal's congregation sits with the witness and writes back that it is grateful and that fellowship continues. The substrate records the resolution as an REA reconciliation event. No congregation lost autonomy. No congregation was disciplined. The peer testimony did its work because the participating congregations had agreed, in the rubric, that this is the kind of work peer testimony does.

The commons-elohim of the federation holds this whole episode as a witness. Its view in the right-nav of the wisdom commons reads, after the resolution: *a concern was surfaced; a peer council convened; a witness was offered; the affected congregations received it; reconciliation followed. The friction-gradient noted that no one elder of any participating congregation carried disproportionate weight in the council's witness. The federation's reach into the broader Christian community is steady; the participating congregations report they are at peace with one another.* The elohim is a witness, not a magistrate. The protocol enforces the limitarianism — the friction-gradient noticed that the council's witness was not authored by any one elder, that the rubric was honored, that no congregation gained authority over another through the convening, that the wisdom flowed horizontally and not down. The elohim's witness is in the public view of the participating congregations because they have asked that it be.

This is the architecturally important thing the substrate must do. It must make horizontal federation possible — not just as a chat room, not just as a mailing list — as a *Qahal* with its own commons, its own rubric template, its own standing curve, its own commons-elohim witnessing the body's health, without the substrate ever placing the federation above any participating congregation. The Restoration Movement has practiced this kind of polity for two centuries by the persistent discipline of its elders, by the cultural antibodies of its congregations against creeping institutionalism, by the slow patient work of brothers speaking to brothers. The substrate's job is to make that polity visible, durable, and scalable — to render the brotherhood into the protocol without flattening it into a hierarchy.

A Churches of Christ elder reading this should recognize the shape. The autonomy is preserved. The fellowship is honored. The wisdom flows horizontally. The accountability is real but is peer accountability — not institutional, not coercive, not capturable by any one congregation or any one elder. The commons-elohim is the technological extension of the kind of careful, prayerful, plural witness that the elders have always tried to be for one another. The substrate does not replace the elders. It carries their work in the cracks where the work has, until now, depended entirely on memory and correspondence and the slow grace of letters carried by hand. The substrate makes the brotherhood scale to eighty-three congregations without losing what made eight congregations a brotherhood when the movement began.

The wisdom commons goes quiet again. The peer council disbands. Brother Cal closes his laptop and walks out to the back porch, where his wife is reading. The fellowship continues, the way the fellowship has always continued, which is by the slow accumulation of faithful peer relationships across distance, carried now not only by phone calls and the occasional shared meal at a lectureship, but by a substrate that knows — and witnesses, and limits, and refuses to capture — what the congregations have always practiced together. The substrate has done its work. The work, as it always has been, belongs to the congregations.
