## Introduction

This essay examines how past decisions about communication technologies shaped society differently across nations, and argues for building public service digital media as an alternative to surveillance-based platform capitalism.

## Radio 1912-1927: The Making of "This New Noise"

The author traces radio's rapid evolution from Marconi's 1894 wireless experiments to its cultural dominance by the late 1920s. Within fifteen years of becoming practical in 1912, radio transformed from a technical curiosity into a powerful medium reshaping society.

Three nations pursued distinctly different radio models. The United States experienced chaotic competition among hundreds of broadcasters—churches, universities, and department stores—before commercial consolidation. AT&T's 1922 "toll broadcasting" model established advertising-supported radio. By 1934, NBC and CBS controlled 70 percent of advertising revenue, with networked programming dominating the landscape.

The Soviet Union weaponized radio for state propaganda. As industrialization progressed, loudspeakers in public squares spread "Radioperedacha," the national broadcaster's messaging. Later, "wired radios" with single-channel hardwired speakers gave citizens no tuning capability, maximizing state control.

The United Kingdom's model differed fundamentally. The Post Office established the BBC as a monopoly with guaranteed revenue from receiver license fees. Director John Reith declared radio should serve as citizens' "guide, philosopher, and friend," prioritizing information, education, and entertainment—in that order.

Hilda Matheson, BBC's first Director of Talks, pioneered original reporting and the modern interview format. Despite serving MI5 during wartime, she championed amplifying underheard voices. The BBC's fiscal independence from advertisers enabled ambitious programming impossible under market pressure.

These three models demonstrate that technology alone doesn't determine social outcomes. Political choices, economic structures, and cultural values shape how nations adopt innovations.

## Three Models for the Internet

The commercialization of the internet beginning around 1995 replicated this pattern of divergent paths.

**The American Model:** Display advertising became the default business model. Early sites like Geocities and Tripod generated pageviews through user-created content. When advertisers discovered display ads underperformed, companies began collecting detailed behavioral data. This surveillance model elevated Google and Facebook to dominance, controlling 59.3% of global ad spending. These platforms direct attention itself—the currency of media—giving them unprecedented power over publishers and public discourse.

**The Chinese Model:** Government censorship of foreign platforms paradoxically protected China's domestic internet ecosystem from American competition. Rather than sterile propaganda, Chinese online culture developed creative resistance, with netizens employing visual vocabularies and wordplay to express forbidden ideas within censorship constraints. WeChat evolved beyond simple messaging into a comprehensive ecosystem—marketplace, payment system, application platform—demonstrating capabilities Facebook can only aspire toward. This represents hypercapitalist monopoly concentration exceeding American models, combining state control with corporate dominance.

**The Wikipedia Model:** Outside China, Wikipedia ranks consistently in the top ten global websites despite accepting no advertising, conducting no user tracking, and selling no data. Wikimedia operates on an $80 million annual budget—roughly 0.25% of Facebook's spending—relying primarily on individual donations. Wikipedia's volunteer editors spend dozens of hours weekly refining articles through robust debate until reaching consensus or requiring administrative intervention. This recognizes user labor as valued contribution rather than exploitable product. Wikimedia represents public service media resembling American public radio—supported through donations rather than license fees—driven by articulated values about knowledge access rather than market signals.

## Newt Minow and the "Vast Wasteland"

When Newt Minow became FCC chair in 1961, television executives braced for regulation. Minow challenged broadcasters: "sit down in front of your television set... and stay there for a day without a book, without a magazine... Keep your eyes glued to the set... what you will observe is a vast wasteland."

Though his term was brief, Minow's legacy proved substantial. The 1961 All-Channel Receiver Act mandated UHF capability for creative experimentation and public service media. The 1962 Communications Satellite Act enabled wireless syndication for public broadcasters. By 1967, President Johnson established the Corporation for Public Broadcasting, creating PBS and NPR.

Rather than displacing commercial media, public broadcasting filled market gaps. Shows like Sesame Street addressed educational programming shortages. More recently, as political polarization increased, public media's importance became evident. University of Missouri research identified local public television stations, NPR, and PBS as most-trusted American media brands.

Public broadcasting arrived decades after commercial radio and television established dominance. This demonstrates that introducing public service media as market-failure correction is viable long after a medium matures.

## Principles for Public Service Digital Media

**Publicly Spirited, But Diverse in Funding:** Public service digital media fill unfilled market gaps while strengthening democratic societies. They inform users, enable assembly and deliberation, facilitate civic mobilization, and provide transparency about information-sharing and algorithmic control. Business model sustainability matters less than values and goals—Mozilla Firefox exemplifies profitable public-spirited service.

**Plural in Purpose:** Facebook's one-size-fits-all approach proves inappropriate for diverse contexts. Bahraini activist Esra'a al Shafei created Majal specifically for Arab LGBTQ teens, enforcing anonymity and cartoon avatars while restricting commenting privileges. These rules appropriately address surveilled, persecuted communities but wouldn't suit casual social connection. Public service tools should support diverse use cases like WordPress—flexible publishing platforms serving varied needs—rather than pursuing market dominance through scale.

**Participatory in Governance:** Reddit demonstrates how volunteer moderators, investing dozens of hours weekly, enforce community-specific rules while training participants in democratic self-governance. Communities learn decision-making through setting guidelines and discussing their enforcement. Unlike Facebook's centralized moderation, multiple platforms with distinct rulesets enable experimentation in community management.

**Publicly Auditable and Reviewable:** Facebook's 61-million-person 2012 experiment demonstrated users exposed to voting reminders from friends were 0.39% more likely to vote. In close elections, such effects could theoretically prove decisive. Yet no mechanism exists to audit whether platforms suppress particular political viewpoints or manipulate election outcomes. Public service digital media should be engineered from inception for audit compliance and algorithmic transparency, enabling independent scholarly review while protecting user privacy.

## What Could We Build?

The ecosystem lacks sufficient engineers and entrepreneurs designing civic-need tools rather than market-driven products. Several possibilities emerge:

**Auditable and Transparent Search and Discovery:** Opaque ranking algorithms exercise tremendous power—moving off Google's first page devastates online referrals and sales. Yet Google refuses algorithm disclosure, fearing gaming. Building entirely new search engines represents monumental undertakings. More viable approaches target specific niches. Transparent search impact might not produce nonprofit Google competitors but rather strategies enabling independent verification of unbiased search.

**Non-Surveillant Advertising Networks:** Google AdSense dominates webpage monetization through combining AdSense tracking with Analytics and DoubleClick data, creating surveillance presence on roughly 80% of the web. Alternative models could target ads based on webpage content parsing combined with loose geodata targeting users to their city rather than street address. This enables content monetization without user surveillance. Podcast sponsorship's success—despite minimal behavioral information—suggests market opportunity for non-surveillant advertising.

**Alternative Social Networks:** Facebook and Twitter's dominance aggravates problems including misinformation prevalence, polarization, and echo chambers. Their scale means interventions succeeding in Myanmar transfer to Maryland. Their opacity prevents non-Facebook entities from addressing Facebook's problems. Alternative platforms designed for geographic communities and communities of interest could introduce novel capabilities. Realizing diverse social media communities requires technical advances—single sign-on enabling easy multi-network participation without Google/Facebook login dependency; aggregation supporting one-interface access across networks; interoperability ensuring new content types remain accessible to aggregators—plus legal framework changes.

## Funding and First Steps

One funding mechanism involves taxing surveillance-intensive advertising. The global digital advertising market totals $333 billion annually. A 1% levy on highly surveillant advertising could generate $1-2 billion annually for public service digital media support. This figure exceeds Wikimedia's $91 million budget by twenty-two times and Mozilla's $56 million by thirty-six times, enabling substantial innovation.

Alternative funding uses include supporting systemic digital media effects research. Substantial scholarly disagreement exists regarding social media's addictive effects, polarization acceleration, extremism promotion, and democratic subversion. These research questions require vast datasets demanding costly server infrastructure and programming expertise.

Immediate action steps include:

**Legislative interoperability requirements:** Rather than data portability focusing on user export capabilities, interoperability might require major platforms maintaining APIs enabling third-party tools for multi-platform interaction.

**Philanthropic experimentation funding:** While defensive work addressing surveillance and platform power proves critical, this requires complementary affirmative vision. Philanthropic and government funders should support diverse experiments around special-purpose networks, specialized search engines, alternative revenue generation, and other digital public services.

**Research agenda development:** Rather than regulating platforms based on uncertain science, governments and philanthropies should fund rigorous research about social media effects on individual and civic health.

**Surveillance advertising taxation pilots:** Introducing surveillant advertising taxation within EU countries could establish proof-of-concept funding mechanisms.

## Conclusion

The fundamental point transcends specific proposals: excessive imagination failure characterizes current debates about digital futures. We readily critique platform shortcomings yet rarely propose serious alternatives, effectively ceding the internet's future to entrenched companies.

Public service digital media offers opportunities for genuinely reimagining digital possibilities. The question facing societies is fundamental: "What is the media environment we want?"

The essay ultimately invites readers beyond defensive critique toward affirmative vision-building about what democratic digital infrastructure might accomplish.
