# Which abundance, from the demand side

*The operator's reflection, written after church on 2026-10-11 and reproduced verbatim as §6. This
note sits beside canon's [§5.6 "Which abundance?"](epr:succession) and the open question the
[monetary posture](epr:monetary-posture-internal-currencies-external-fiat-2026-08-23) §8 carries as
"Which abundance". Canon answers from the **supply** side: some building raises what the land can
carry and some building uses it up. This note supplies the **demand** side. How much must be
produced depends on how much we want, so what we want decides how much work there is. The two
answers meet at a word both traditions already use: **enough**.*

**Grading** (this directory's legend): ✅ verified in source this pass · ◐ single-source or
recalled-canonical, not re-verified this pass · ⚠ verify at source before it carries weight.
Scripture is cited by reference and quoted from memory in a standard translation (ESV unless
noted). Read it as ◐ until a reader checks the wording.

## 0. The first beat: the garden

Scripture's economy opens in a garden and closes in a garden-city. Everything in this note sits
between those two scenes, and each section can be read as one step from the first toward the
second.

**What the garden gives (Genesis 1–2).** Provision comes before labour: "I have given you every
plant… for food" (1:29). Rest comes before work: God rests on the seventh day (2:2-3), and the
humans made on the sixth begin their life on a day of rest, not of work. Work is real, but its shape
is care: the man is placed in the garden "to work it and keep it" (2:15). The Hebrew pair *ʿābad /
šāmar*, serve and guard, describes tending, not extraction. And the abundance has a limit inside
it: every tree is free but one (2:16-17). **The first economy is gift, rest, keeping, and a
boundary that makes "enough" possible.** ◐

**What grasping costs (Genesis 3).** The fall in this telling is an act of wanting: the tree was
"good for food… a delight to the eyes… to be desired" (3:6). What follows is the curse on work:
"cursed is the ground because of you; in pain you shall eat of it… by the sweat of your face"
(3:17-19). **Toil is what grasping costs.** Work stops being keeping and becomes compulsion. That is
§2's claim in its oldest form: when wanting outruns the boundary, the work it compels becomes a
burden. The story then moves to the first city, built by Cain (4:17), and to Babel's bricks
(11:3), the first economy of production for its own sake. ◐

**What the law and the wilderness carry forward.** The manna (§1) is the garden's rule carried into
the wilderness: daily gift, decay instead of hoarding, and a Sabbath portion. Sabbath, the
seventh-year release and Jubilee (§4) are the garden's rule written into law for a people who now
hold land: return the land, release the debt, rest the soil, so that no one is shut out of the
garden for good. Gesell's demurrage (§1) is a modern attempt at the same rule. ◐

## 1. Why demurrage: money kept in relationship with goods

Canon already states the mechanism ✅ ([succession §3.1–3.2](epr:succession)): *money can wait, and
goods cannot.* Grain spoils, tools rust, buildings need roofs. Money alone costs nothing to hold, so
whoever holds money has leverage over everyone who holds goods or sells hours. Gesell's *Freigeld*
puts a carrying cost on money "to put money on the same footing as the goods it is exchanged
against."

The operator's addition is a reason, not a new mechanism. **Demurrage is relational.** A unit of
money stands for goods and labour that decay, and money that does not decay stops standing for
them. It turns into a separate thing with its own interests, which are the interests of whoever
holds it. A carrying cost keeps the representation honest: money ages the way the things it stands
for age. That is why it moves in a circle and does not pool. Circulation is the visible effect. The
cause is that the symbol never comes loose from the thing.

The scriptural form of this is older than Gesell and more exact:

- **Exodus 16:18-20.** "Whoever gathered much had nothing left over, and whoever gathered little had
  no lack… some left part of it till the morning, and it bred worms and stank." The manna decayed at
  the rate of daily need. Hoarding was not forbidden, it was impossible. ◐
- **Exodus 16:22-26.** The double portion gathered on the sixth day "did not stink, and there were
  no worms in it." **Keeping something for the Sabbath was allowed, and the manna was made to
  last.** The monetary posture states the same line in its own terms, in §2.2: *preservation is
  legitimate; accretion is not*. Exodus is more specific about which preservation counts: **storing
  up so that people can rest.** ◐
- **Matthew 6:19-21.** "Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust
  destroy… For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also." Jesus treats decay as the
  plain nature of earthly treasure. He does not offer it as a threat. ◐
- **James 5:1-4.** "Your gold and silver have corroded, and their corrosion will be evidence
  against you… Behold, the wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you kept back by
  fraud, are crying out against you." **Here the rust is a witness, and what it testifies to is
  withheld wages.** Hoarding and underpayment are one sin seen from the two ends of the same
  transaction. ◐

**Take (study).** A currency lens that declares a carrying cost should also say **which goods'
decay it follows**. The cost could be fitted to the depreciation of the basket the unit clears, or
it could be an arbitrary rate. The monetary posture's five knobs (§2.3) carry the carry cost as a
bare number. Declaring what the number refers to would make Gesell's reasoning something a
council can examine, not a parameter it simply accepts or rejects. That matches the trap-detector
rule: show the mechanism, not just the aggregate. No row is minted. This is a refinement for
whoever next writes the carry-cost knob.

## 2. Abundance compels the work

The operator's second observation:

> How much is needed is directly connected with how much we produce… so by creating "abundance" we
> are creating the goods and services that COMPEL the work… our own desires are the thing that puts
> the pressure on society to put in overtime.

This is the part canon's §5.6 leaves out. The extensive/intensive table asks *what kind* of
building. It does not ask **how much wanting the building is for**, and the corpus treats
abundance, in Proudhon's way, as the weapon against rent. The weapon is real. But abundance pursued
as the endless multiplication of goods has three costs that never appear on the ledger as costs:

1. **Production uses up people as well as land.** Every new want is a claim on somebody's hours, and
   very often on the hours of the people least able to refuse: the service worker, the farm worker,
   the caregiver, the night shift. Meadows' harvest/regeneration ratio
   ([measure-family row 13](epr:measure-family-borrows-backlog)) applies to a person exactly as it
   applies to a fishery. **Overtime is harvest above the rate at which people regenerate**, and like
   any overshoot it wears down the capacity it draws on, in health, family and attention. ◐
2. **The floor rises with the ceiling.** When what everyone else consumes goes up, the cost of
   *taking part* goes up with it: two cars to hold an entry-level job, a smartphone plan, rent in a
   place where those jobs are. Each new comfort for the comfortable becomes a requirement for the
   squeezed. This is the hedonic treadmill operating as a structure, not as a personal failing.
   (Schor's *The Overspent American* is the standard empirical treatment ⚠.)
3. **Efficiency does not save the hours.** Jevons and the rebound effect: when something gets
   cheaper to produce, people use more of it, and the saved time and energy are spent rather than
   rested. Keynes in 1930 predicted a fifteen-hour week for his grandchildren (*Economic
   Possibilities for our Grandchildren* ◐). The productivity arrived and the leisure did not. **We
   chose more over enough, and someone else's overtime paid for it.**

**The claim this supports, for the manifesto:** *abundance is not the most a system can produce. It
is a sufficiency that people can actually rest in.* An economy that cannot say "enough" turns every
gain in productivity into more required labour. An economy that can say it turns the same gain into
Sabbath. The test of abundance is not how much there is, but whether **those who labour, including
mothers, caretakers, farm workers, service workers and volunteers, are rested, paid, and not
poor.**

## 3. Contentment is an input, and it cannot be required by rule

The operator's third line is the one the mechanism cannot provide:

> Economic justice is only achievable through our own humility to find a wealth beyond the material.

This is the *metanoia* condition. Gesell, George and Proudhon each built an instrument that works on
the supply of money, land or capital. None of them could act on *wanting*, and all of them assumed
it would stay the same. Canon's §5.5 already names the **discernment** that the blunt instruments
stood in for. This note names a second input that no mechanism produces: **contentment**, the
ability of a person or a community to recognise *enough* and stop there.

Two consequences for the protocol, both consistent with positions already taken:

- **Contentment cannot be imposed by a lens.** A floor or ceiling lens can make the cost of
  overshoot visible and can make rest affordable. A rule that requires people to be content is
  austerity, and austerity always falls on the people who already have the least. This repeats the
  monetary posture's §2.8 ("the equilibrium is a discernment, never a denomination") at the scale
  of desire. **Contentment is practised. It is never legislated.**
- **What the protocol *can* do is make rest legible.** Today the only abundance that shows up on a
  ledger is output. A REA ledger that records the labour time *and the rest given up* to produce a
  resource lets a community see what its abundance cost and who paid. The take that survives is a
  measure, minted below.

Sahlins' "original affluent society" (*Stone Age Economics*, 1972 ◐) is the anthropological
version: the "Zen road to affluence" reaches plenty by wanting little, not by producing much. The
Christian version is older: Philippians 4:11-12, "I have learned in whatever situation I am to be
content"; 1 Timothy 6:6-10, "godliness with contentment is great gain"; Hebrews 13:5. ◐

### 4.1 Real finances mind the gap

The operator's follow-on note, the same day:

> Real finances consider not just what we earn, but consequentially how we live relative to your
> median neighbor, finances that are "real" minds the gap. Holding this thought seriously is what
> would separate the economics of heaven from the corruption laundered in with the popular but
> captured stories of "Financial Peace".

This turns §4 around. §4 asked whether the pulpit knows the pew's finances. This asks what counts
as a finance at all. The usual household picture has two numbers: what comes in (income) and what
is owned net of what is owed (net worth). **Real finances add a third: the gap**, meaning how a
household lives compared with its median neighbor, and whose hours that margin requires. A balance
sheet can be healthy while the gap widens, and on this reading that household is not at peace. It
is drifting away from its neighbor and calling the drift security.

Scripture keeps this third number. The rich fool of Luke 12:16-21 has excellent finances by the
first two measures, with a surplus and a plan for bigger barns, and God calls him a fool because he
is "not rich toward God." Paul's *isotēs* (2 Cor 8:13-15) measures between households, not inside
one. Amos 6:4-6 condemns those who lie on ivory beds "but are not grieved over the ruin of Joseph":
their sin is the distance and the indifference to it. ◐

**Financial Peace, read fairly and then for what it leaves out.** Dave Ramsey's *Financial Peace
University* and *The Total Money Makeover* are the most widely taught household-finance curriculum
in American churches ◐. The "baby steps" go: a starter emergency fund, the debt snowball, three to
six months of expenses saved, 15% invested for retirement, college savings, paying off the house,
then "build wealth and give." The tagline is "live like no one else, so later you can live and give
like no one else" ◐. **What it gets right deserves saying first.** Consumer debt is a real bondage
(Proverbs 22:7, "the borrower is the slave of the lender," is its central verse), and the
programme has helped many households get out of it. The discipline, the intention and the refusal
of debt-funded consumption are all sound.

What the operator calls *laundered in*, graded as a critique and not as fact:

| What the story assumes | What it leaves out | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| The unit of finance is the household alone | Structural extraction (rent on land the community made valuable, stagnant wages, medical debt, payday lending) appears only as a discipline problem for the person paying it | ◐ critique |
| Proverbs 22:7 is read as advice to the *borrower* | The same canon's usury law addresses the *lender* (§4). The system that profits from the bondage is never called to repentance. The debtor is only taught to escape it, one household at a time | ◐ critique |
| The end state is accumulation, "live like no one else" | It is Matthew 6:19 deferred: treasure on earth, later. "Like no one else" measures success by distance *above* the neighbor, which is the gap renamed as a reward | ◐ critique |
| Peace is measured on one's own balance sheet | The gap is invisible to the measure, so a household can reach "financial peace" while its neighbor's position worsens, partly because of the same system its investments are in | ◐ critique |
| Giving is a step or a percentage | Giving is not tied to the gap; Deuteronomy's floor (§4) and Paul's *isotēs* measure giving by the neighbor's need, not one's own milestone | ◐ critique |

**The evidence companion: Matthew Desmond, *Poverty, by America* (2023) ◐.** Desmond's thesis is
the gap read from the other side. Poverty persists in a rich country because the non-poor benefit
from it: as employers of low-wage labor, as consumers of cheap goods and services, as landlords and
lenders who profit from the poor paying more for housing and credit, and as recipients of tax
breaks that dwarf aid to the poor. His prescription, to become a *poverty abolitionist* who will not
profit from a neighbor's poverty, is the operator's essay line "our own material success must never
require another person's poverty" in policy form. The course puts it first on Module 12's reading
list.

**The take.** Gap-aware finance is not a new instrument. It is the **personal equilibrium** that the
monetary posture §2.8 and canon §5.5 already name: what right relationship with what one holds
requires, found by negotiation and never set by a number. The gap is the evidence that negotiation
reads. It stays a discernment, never a denomination (§3): no household is graded by its distance
from the median, because that would be the observer trap. But a community that **can see the gap**
can ask the question Financial Peace's measure cannot ask, which is *peace with whom?*

### 4.2 The closing beat: the garden-city

The story does not end by going back to Eden. It ends in a city with the garden inside it, which
answers each of the Genesis losses:

- **The curse on work is lifted.** "No longer will there be anything accursed… and his servants will
  worship him" (Rev 22:3). The verb for service is the garden's word again: work restored as keeping,
  not toil. ◐
- **Abundance comes as a flow, and it is shared.** The tree of life bears "fruit each month" and its
  leaves are "for the healing of the nations" (22:2). Fruit that comes every month needs no barn.
  Hoarding has nothing to protect. ◐
- **There are no gates to keep anyone out.** "Its gates will never be shut by day" (21:25). The
  essay's "gated suburbs" are the opposite of this city. ◐
- **Labour is not taken by someone else.** "They shall build houses and inhabit them… they shall not
  build and another inhabit" (Isaiah 65:21-23). That is a world without rent extraction and with
  fair wages. "Each under his vine and fig tree, and no one shall make them afraid" (Micah 4:4) is
  sufficiency held in security, not accumulation, and it follows directly on swords beaten into
  ploughshares. ◐
- **Its counterpart is named.** Babylon's merchants grieve over a cargo list that ends "slaves, that
  is, human souls" (Rev 18:13). That is an economy whose abundance requires someone's poverty, judged
  at the end of the canon. ◐

**This is where the operator's phrase "exiting wealth into a garden community" points.** It does
not mean retreating to a smallholding. It means moving one's holdings out of the Babylon pattern of
extraction and into the garden-city pattern of keeping, sharing and rest, now, as a foretaste. The
church's local economy (§4) and the protocol's commons are both attempts to live in the second city
before it arrives. The gap (§4.1) measures how far a household still lives in the first.

**Where this goes:** Module 12 of *Foundations for Christians and Technology* now carries the
tithe-as-floor, usury and "real finances mind the gap" readings, closing on the garden-city; Module 7, opening from the garden, carries the manna, rust and Sabbath reading of
demurrage (§7 below). The course states these in its own voice. The essay stays here as the
operator's own words.

## 5. The next link on the map: degrowth (study, not yet surveyed)

The obvious neighbour this note touches but does not read is **degrowth / post-growth economics**.
It is recorded as the next exploration so the link exists before the survey does. The questions a
survey should answer for *this* corpus:

1. **Degrowth against Proudhon's weapon.** Canon says to build until the scarcity that yields rent
   is gone. Degrowth says to produce less. Hickel's *selective* degrowth (shrink the unnecessary,
   meaning SUVs, planned obsolescence and advertising, and grow care, education and public goods)
   looks very like canon's intensive column. Is the conflict real, or a matter of vocabulary? ⚠
2. **Who degrows.** A poor household cannot be asked to want less. The survey must keep
   sufficiency for those above the floor separate from deprivation for those below it. This is the
   austerity trap from §3, and degrowth writers argue about it among themselves.
3. **Chosen versus imposed.** Kate Soper's *alternative hedonism* (*Post-Growth Living*, 2020 ◐)
   argues that a post-growth life is *more* pleasurable, not a sacrifice. That is the secular
   relative of "wealth beyond the material", and the closest bridge-legible partner to §3.
4. **Sabbath as economics.** Brueggemann's *Sabbath as Resistance* (2014 ◐) reads Pharaoh's brick
   quotas against the fourth commandment: Sabbath as refusing an economy with no "enough". Heschel's
   *The Sabbath* (1951 ◐) is the theological ground. Both connect to Module 7's Maccabean
   counter-story.
5. **Rebound.** Does a degrowth programme have an answer to Jevons other than willpower? If it does
   not, contentment (§3) carries the weight and the protocol's job is to make that weight visible.

Reading list for the survey (unread this pass): Jason Hickel, *Less Is More* (2020); Giorgos Kallis
et al., *The Case for Degrowth* (2020); Serge Latouche, *Farewell to Growth* (2009); Tim Jackson,
*Prosperity without Growth* (2009/2017); Kate Soper, *Post-Growth Living* (2020); Ivan Illich,
*Tools for Conviviality* (1973) and *Energy and Equity* (1974); E. F. Schumacher, *Small Is
Beautiful* (1973), ch. "Buddhist Economics"; Juliet Schor, *Plenitude* (2010); Herman Daly,
*Steady-State Economics* (1977); the Post-Growth Institute (already on Module 7's shelf). The
survey belongs in the Research Index's "Limits Problem" section, beside Meadows, since *Limits to
Growth* is degrowth's founding text.
