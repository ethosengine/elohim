# Comparative Political Economy — a reading program

**What this is.** A directed reading list in comparative political economy and African development
economics, assembled to answer one question the protocol cannot answer from inside itself: *when
mishpat care activity — care work witnessed and notarized under the protocol's justice pillar —
aggregates in a place, what does the measure we form carry into that place that we did not put
there on purpose?*

The proximate provocation is the Nordic social democracies — the standing candidate for "a society
that made it," and therefore the standing candidate for a model to encode. The reading exists to
test whether that outcome is a **portable mechanism** or a **local accident**, and to name, in
advance, the traps that a signal-forming substrate walks into when it arrives in Texas, Alaska,
Kenya, Guatemala, or Norway — this document's recurring stand-ins for the range of places the
substrate could land — carrying assumptions built somewhere else.

**The system in question, for a reader arriving cold.** The Elohim Protocol is a distributed
platform for learning, care, and community governance; this document concerns its measurement
layer. Its primitive is a *witnessed care event*: a record of one concrete act of care — who gave
it, who received it, what resource flowed, the story around it — attested by the participants and
cryptographically notarized on a shared, tamper-evident data layer rather than in any institution's
private database. "The substrate" names that shared storage-and-aggregation layer; "signal
formation" names the step where many such events are composed into aggregate measures; a
"deployment context" is a particular place and community the protocol operates in. *Elohim
councils* are the local deliberative bodies that read those aggregates and decide what the
community does about them — funding, priorities, repair. Every "signal implication" below is
addressed to the people designing what that aggregation computes and what those councils are shown.

**Method note and provenance, stated up front.** This is a reading *program*, not a survey of read
sources. It was assembled on 2026-08-07 by the project's AI research agent working from secondary
(training-corpus) knowledge under the operator's direction; nothing was verified at source in that
sitting. The propositions in §2 are graded by **author confidence from secondary knowledge**;
grades appear on the §2 propositions and on numeric claims, while the traps in §4 and the per-book
weakness notes in §6 are **ungraded author judgment** and should be read as such. Grades mean:
✅ well-established and not seriously contested · ◐ contested with real literature on both sides ·
⚠ a figure or claim to check at the source before it is load-bearing. The point of the grades is to
tell the reader *where to press*, not to license citation. Every ⚠ is a homework item; the marks
are left in place rather than collected, and the first reading pass should clear them.

**How to read it.** §1–§5 are the analytic frame — read these first, they are what makes the books
pay. §6 is the reading itself: five core, then four tracks. §7 is the standing constraint the whole
exercise serves. The closing Outputs section records what this document hands to the design backlog
and why not yet. The reading is executed opportunistically by the operator and the project's
research agents — no deadline is set, and §4 is usable as-is meanwhile, with grades upgraded as
sources are actually read. (The frontmatter `status: Capture` is this directory's state word for
exactly that: research captured and usable, not yet driving design.)

## 1. The question, stated so the reading can answer it

**The plain version.** Is the Nordic outcome a thing you can build toward, or a thing that happened
to a specific people under conditions that no longer exist and could not be reproduced without doing
things we would refuse to do?

**The substrate version — the one that actually matters here.** The protocol's bet is that
micro-level care activity, witnessed and notarized as it happens, can aggregate into context rich
enough that elohim councils form *good policy* from it. That is a bridge between two altitudes:

- **Atomic in micro** — a single witnessed act of care, with its participants, its resource flow,
  its story. Legible, local, unarguable.
- **Structural in macro** — the shape of a whole political economy: who holds the rent, who is
  inside the boundary of membership, whose labor is priced below what it produces, which
  institutions existed before the windfall arrived.

The hard part is that **the second is not a sum of the first.** No quantity of witnessed care events
in Guatemala tells you that the terms of trade are set elsewhere. No quantity of witnessed care
events in Norway tells you that the fiscal capacity underwriting them was consolidated before the oil
was found. A substrate that aggregates faithfully and stops there produces a beautifully-measured
picture of a system whose actual determinants are entirely off-frame.

**This is the reading's real job:** the comparative literature is a century of people building the
two halves of exactly that bridge — micro measures here, macro structure there — without ever
completing the join. Knowing precisely *where* each attempt stops is worth more than any of their
conclusions, because the stopping points are where our measure design has to be deliberate rather
than default.

## 2. Three propositions to adjudicate

Stated as propositions to be tested by the reading, with current state of evidence. None of these is
settled; the grades say how contested.

### Proposition A — the trust was manufactured, and the manufacturing is the point

*Claim: Nordic high-trust universalism was made possible in part by a boundary on membership that
the state actively policed, rather than by a homogeneity the state happened to inherit.*

**Grade: ✅ on the historical record; ◐ on the causal weight.**

The historical record is not in dispute. Norwegianization (*fornorsking*) of the Sámi ran from the
1850s into the 1960s. Sweden's State Institute for Racial Biology opened at Uppsala in 1922 — the
first state racial-biology institute anywhere. Sterilization programs ran in Sweden from 1935 to
1976 (⚠ on the order of 60,000 people; estimates of how many were coerced vary widely and are
politically contested), skewing toward Roma, Travellers, and poor women. Denmark's record in
Greenland includes the 1951 "experiment children" and an IUD campaign conducted on Greenlandic girls
without consent into the 1970s.

So the *folkhem* — the people's home — had a people, and the boundary of that people was drawn and
enforced. Whether that boundary was **causally necessary** to the welfare state is the contested part.

The Alesina–Glaeser thesis (*Fighting Poverty in the US and Europe*, 2004) — that ethnic
fractionalization predicts lower redistribution — is the canonical statement, and it has taken real
damage. Banting & Kymlicka's work finds no robust "progressive's dilemma" (the claimed trade-off
between diversity and redistributive solidarity). The better-supported
reading is that diversity as such does not erode solidarity; **political entrepreneurs racializing
distribution do.** That makes the operative variable constructed and contestable rather than
demographic — which is either encouraging or alarming depending on who is doing the constructing.

*Why this matters to us:* any trust metric that rewards homogeneity of the participating set will
reproduce the boundary silently. See Trap 4.

### Proposition B — the European welfare state was underwritten by colonial extraction

*Claim: Northern European prosperity, including its social provision, is constituted by net
appropriation from the periphery — past and ongoing.*

**Grade: ◐ overall, and the grade splits sharply by which version is being asserted.**

The strong form (Rodney, Amin, and their contemporary heirs) does badly on Nordic geography
specifically. Sweden and Norway held essentially no colonies. Norway was itself a periphery of
Denmark and then Sweden until 1905 and was poor; its takeoff runs through shipping, hydropower, and
fish before oil. Finland was a Russian grand duchy and remained poor into the 1950s. Denmark is the
genuine exception in the group — Caribbean plantation slavery, Tranquebar, Greenland. Choosing the
Nordics as the case for "welfare = colonial loot" picks the weakest available ground.

The sophisticated form survives that objection and is where the reading should concentrate:

1. **Participation in a colonial system without owning colonies** — Swedish iron and ball bearings,
   Norwegian shipping carrying colonial trade.
2. **Postwar integration into a division of labor whose terms were set by colonial-era
   institutions** — the point Ha-Joon Chang makes without needing any value-theory commitment at all.
3. **Internal colonialism** — the Sámi case, which is Proposition A and Proposition B being the same
   fact viewed from two directions.
4. **Ongoing, specific, still-operating arrangements** — the CFA franc is the case where the answer
   is unambiguous and checkable rather than systemic-vague: fourteen African countries, euro-pegged,
   reserves historically held at the French Treasury. This is not a nineteenth-century artifact.

The **measurement** is where the strong and sophisticated forms diverge most usefully for us.
Unequal-exchange accounting (⚠ Hickel et al. put net drain from the Global South at roughly $2.2
trillion/yr; ⚠ Patnaik's British-drain-from-India figure of ~$45 trillion for 1765–1938) works by
asserting that price ≠ value and then imputing value by embodied labor or material content. Critics
argue this assumes what it sets out to demonstrate and cannot separate appropriation from
productivity difference. By contrast, Ndikumana & Boyce's capital-flight work measures **observable
mechanisms** — trade mis-invoicing, offshore accounts, odious debt service — and is far more
defensible for it.

*That divergence is a measure-design lesson, not a political one, and it is the single most
transferable thing in this section.* See Trap 7.

### Proposition C — austerity is a political instrument, not a technical response

*Claim (Clara Mattei, `The Capital Order`, 2022): interwar austerity in Britain and Italy was chosen
to dismantle worker-control experiments and restore capital's disciplinary position, not to solve a
debt problem.*

**Grade: ✅ for the archival claim as scoped; ◐ for the generalization; and the colonial corollary is
inherited rather than researched.**

Mattei is an economist working in the heterodox tradition, now directing a heterodox-economics
center at the University of Tulsa (◐ on the exact affiliation and date — worth confirming). The book's force comes from a specific
methodological choice: it is **archival**. Treasury and Bank of England papers, the Brussels (1920)
and Genoa (1922) conferences, Italian state records. In those documents policymakers describe the
disciplinary purpose in their own words. Where certainty in this literature is usually theoretical,
hers on this point is documentary — that is the honest answer to "where does the confidence come
from," and it is a good answer for the claim as scoped.

Three things a careful reader should hold separately:

- **The scoped claim** — interwar Britain and Italy, austerity as class discipline. Strong, and the
  archives carry it.
- **The generalization** — austerity as a trans-historical instrument. This flattens 1920s
  gold-standard deflation, 1970s IMF conditionality, and 2010s eurozone fiscal consolidation into
  one object, and the monetary-regime debate (was the return to gold technically motivated, if
  wrongly?) gets less weight than its own literature would give it. Contested, and reasonable people
  land differently.
- **The colonial corollary** — that European social provision is invalidated as evidence by enduring
  extraction. This is Proposition B, and it is **not her archival research**; it is the dependency
  tradition, which she is transmitting rather than establishing. Read it at the source (Rodney,
  Amin, Bhambra) where the evidentiary standards can be judged directly, and grade it there.

*Why this matters to us:* the pattern — an author with genuine documentary authority on a narrow
claim, transmitting a broader inherited frame with the same rhetorical confidence — is not a fault
particular to any one writer. It is the normal shape of scholarship being read outside its own
field, and it is exactly what a substrate that ingests "expert context" for council deliberation
will encounter constantly. **The design lesson is that provenance-of-claim needs to be a
first-class field, not a footnote:** *established-here* vs *transmitted-from-there* is a distinction
a policy-forming pipeline has to be able to carry, because the confidence signal in the prose does
not distinguish them.

## 3. Disaggregation — seven mechanisms, and which travel

"Can we be Norway" is unanswerable and therefore useless. The productive move is to break the model
into separable mechanisms and ask which are portable, which are conditional, and which are accidents.

| # | Mechanism | Portable? | Precondition it hides |
|---|-----------|-----------|----------------------|
| 1 | **Resource-rent capture** — state ownership, high marginal petroleum tax (⚠ ~78% combined in Norway), the Government Pension Fund Global (⚠ well over $1.5T), a spending rule capping draw at ~3% of fund value | Conditional | **Institutions must precede the windfall.** See Trap 1 — this is the sharpest single finding in the whole program |
| 2 | **Pre-tax wage compression** — centralized bargaining, the Rehn-Meidner solidarity wage (Sweden's postwar equal-pay-across-firms doctrine) | Partly | Requires a bargaining counterparty with real coverage; widely missed because analysts look at post-transfer Gini (inequality measured after taxes and transfers) |
| 3 | **Universalism over means-testing** — Korpi & Palme's paradox of redistribution: programs targeted at the poor redistribute *less*, because they shed the middle-class coalition that defends them | **Yes — most portable item on the list** | Requires the middle class to see itself as a beneficiary, not a payer. See Trap 2 |
| 4 | **Decommodified care** — public childcare and eldercare as the enabling condition for labor participation (Esping-Andersen) | Yes | Fiscal capacity, which loops back to 1 |
| 5 | **Flexicurity** — security attached to the worker, not the job (Denmark) | Yes | Active labor-market spending at a level rarely replicated |
| 6 | **State capacity and impartiality** — Rothstein's argument that what reads as "trust" is largely *low corruption plus impartial administration* | Yes, slowly | This is an institutional-quality variable, not an ethnic one — the strongest available counter to Proposition A's causal claim |
| 7 | **Dense prior associational substrate** — cooperatives, folk high schools, free churches, temperance and labor movements, accumulated for a century *before* the welfare state formalized them | **Yes, and this is the one that concerns us most** | Time, and a reason for people to associate that isn't the state |

**Mechanism 7 is the finding this project should sit with.** The Nordic states did not create
associational life; they formalized associational life that already existed and then routed public
provision through and around it. If what gets called "ethnic trust" is substantially *dense prior
associational infrastructure*, then it is **buildable, and it is not ethnic** — which is the most
encouraging result in the literature for anything trying to grow a care economy from the substrate
up rather than legislate one from the top down.

Two disciplines this table imposes on us:

- **No position is taken here on land-value taxation, or on any particular rent-capture doctrine.**
  Mechanism 1 is on the list because the comparative record makes it hard to ignore, not because the
  program endorses a school. The reading should be able to *falsify* it as readily as support it —
  and Trap 1 is in fact a substantial complication for the naive version.
- **The mechanisms are separable, and their portability differs by an order of magnitude.** A
  substrate that ships "the Nordic model" as a single template is shipping seven things bundled,
  six of whose preconditions it has not checked.

## 4. The eight traps

This is the reusable payload — the part that survives after the reading is done. Each is a way the
comparative record says a well-intentioned measure goes wrong, followed by what it implies for
signal formation.

**Trap 1 — Capacity precedes windfall (the Texas/Alaska trap).**
Norway found oil in 1969 and dropped it into an already-consolidated high-capacity state. That state
could therefore impose the tax, the ownership stake, and the fiscal rule. Texas and Oklahoma got the
oil *first* — and built endowments (⚠ the Permanent School Fund and Permanent University Fund are
substantial, roughly $85–95B combined) rather than citizen dividends, capturing a far smaller share
of the rent. Alaska is the American case that did build a permanent fund with a dividend (⚠ ~$75–80B),
and the interesting question is why Alaska and not Texas. Michael Ross's *The Oil Curse* is the
standard treatment of why Norway is the exception rather than the rule.

The bleak implication: **rent capture requires institutional capacity you must already have.** You
cannot bootstrap it from the windfall, because the windfall funds the coalition that prevents you.

→ *Signal implication:* the protocol arriving in a place **after** a value flow is established faces
a categorically different problem than arriving before, and the substrate needs to know which regime
it is in. This should be a detectable state rather than an unknowable one — a hypothesis the design
must test, not a given — and knowing it changes what policy is even available. Make it a first-class question the aggregation can answer, not an assumption.

**Trap 2 — Welfare framed as being for the poor (the targeting trap).**
Korpi & Palme's paradox: the more precisely a program targets the poor, the less it redistributes,
because precise targeting destroys the coalition that funds it. Framing determines durability more
than design does.

→ *Signal implication:* any measure that identifies a class of "beneficiaries" as distinct from a
class of "contributors" manufactures the political fragility it then measures. If mishpat care
activity is surfaced as *need served* rather than *capacity circulating*, the aggregate will read as
a welfare ledger and will attract exactly the politics welfare ledgers attract — regardless of what
the underlying flows actually are.

**Trap 3 — The uniquely-dysfunctional prior.**
Mkandawire's demolition of the "neopatrimonialism" literature makes the point sharply: the analytic
apparatus that gets exported to Africa carries an embedded prior about *what kind of failure to
expect there*, and then finds it. Jerven's work compounds this — much African statistical data is
weak enough that both the pessimist and optimist narratives are constructed on numbers that cannot
bear them, and the "chronic failure" story erases both the 1960s–70s growth period and the post-2000
boom.

→ *Signal implication — the one most likely to bite us:* **an imported measure is an imported
prior.** If the aggregation layer ships with a default schema of what a healthy care economy looks
like, it will grade Kenya against Norway and return an F for reasons that belong to the measure, not
to Kenya. This is the exact failure mode the substrate is most likely to commit and least likely to
notice, because the output will look rigorous.

**Trap 4 — Trust metrics that reward a tight boundary.**
Proposition A's live danger. "Social trust" as a scalar conflates two things that pull in opposite
directions: *associational density* (how much people actually do together) and *boundary tightness*
(how narrowly membership is drawn). A single trust score rises with either.

→ *Signal implication:* decompose trust into at minimum those two components before it feeds
anything. A substrate optimizing an undifferentiated trust signal will discover — correctly, and
catastrophically — that exclusion raises it.

**Trap 5 — Legibility as violence (Scott's trap).**
*Seeing Like a State*: high-modernist schemes fail when they replace local practical knowledge
(*metis*) with a simplified legible grid, because the grid was built for the observer's convenience
and the discarded knowledge was load-bearing. What gets measured is what the center could see, and
the act of making a thing measurable changes it.

→ *Signal implication:* the protocol's core act — witnessing care and making it notarizable — **is a
legibility operation.** It is the same move Scott is warning about, executed with better intentions
and better technology. That does not exempt it. The mitigation is that the schema of what counts as
a witnessed care event must be locally authored and locally revisable, or the protocol becomes the
center whose grid erases the metis.

**Trap 6 — Reading equality in the wrong place.**
Nordic equality is substantially produced *before* taxes, by wage compression (Mechanism 2), not
after them by transfers. An analysis that looks only at redistribution sees a fiscal story and
misses the bargaining story that did most of the work.

→ *Signal implication:* a care-economy measure that counts only flows-to-people misses the structure
that set the flows' baseline. Both altitudes have to be visible or the policy recommendation will
target the smaller lever.

**Trap 7 — Imputed aggregates over observable mechanisms.**
The methodological split inside Proposition B. Unequal-exchange drain figures require imputing value
by a theory; capital-flight figures count mis-invoiced trade and offshore holdings. The second is
smaller, less rhetorically powerful, and far harder to dismiss.

→ *Signal implication, stated as a design rule:* **when a measure can be built either from an
imputed aggregate or from an observable mechanism, build it from the mechanism and let the aggregate
be derived and labeled as such.** A council can argue with a mechanism. It cannot argue with an
aggregate whose value theory is buried three layers down, so it will either accept it whole or
reject it whole — and both are failures of deliberation.

**Trap 8 — Model transfer without precondition checking.**
The synthesis of the §3 table. Every mechanism carries preconditions that the model's *description*
omits, because descriptions are written by people for whom the preconditions were ambient.

→ *Signal implication:* if the substrate ever offers "what worked elsewhere" as council input — and
it will, because that is what rich context looks like — the precondition set has to travel attached
to the mechanism. A recommendation that arrives without its preconditions is not a recommendation;
it is a category error with a citation.

## 5. The bridge problem — where the literature stops

The micro-to-macro bridge in §1 is the protocol's central technical bet, and the honest finding is
that **the comparative literature does not solve it.** Sen gives a defensible micro metric
(capabilities — what a person is actually able to do and be) with no account of the structure that
sets the constraint set. World-systems and dependency theory — the traditions that read the world
economy as a core extracting from a periphery — give a macro structure with no principled descent
to the individual. The two literatures barely cite each other.

Three bodies of work get closer than the rest, and they are the most directly useful reading in the
program for anyone designing the aggregation layer:

- **Alkire & Foster's counting methodology** and the Multidimensional Poverty Index apparatus
  (the Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative, OPHI). This is the closest existing
  technical answer to *"invent measures per-context that
  still aggregate."* The MPI is deliberately built with **locally chosen indicators, cutoffs, and
  weights**, and the counting method is designed so that a locally-parameterized measure remains
  decomposable and comparable without pretending the parameters were universal. Whatever else the
  substrate borrows, it should borrow this: **the parameters are declared, local, and inspectable,
  and the aggregation is honest about being conditional on them.** Primary text: Alkire, Foster,
  Seth, Santos, Roche & Ballon, *Multidimensional Poverty Measurement and Analysis* (2015).
- **Ostrom's social-ecological systems (SES) framework** (*Understanding Institutional Diversity*,
  2005; *Governing the Commons*, 1990). Ostrom's most important methodological commitment is that she refused to produce a model and
  produced a **diagnostic framework** instead — a structured set of questions whose answers differ by
  case, plus design principles that are necessary-ish rather than sufficient. That is precisely the
  posture a protocol operating in Texas and Kenya simultaneously has to adopt, and it is a posture,
  not a feature.
- **Scott's *metis*** as the named category for what the aggregation destroys if it is careless
  (Trap 5), and therefore what the schema has to leave room to carry.

**What none of them do — and what is therefore genuinely open ground rather than a lookup —** is
give an account of how *witnessed micro events*, rather than survey instruments, compose upward into
structural claims. Alkire-Foster composes survey responses. Ostrom composes case studies. Both take
their unit of observation as given, from an instrument designed by an analyst. A substrate whose
micro unit is a **notarized care event with its own provenance, participants, and story** is working
with a materially different primitive, and the composition question for that primitive has not been
answered by anyone in this literature.

That is the strongest reason to do this reading: not to find the answer, but to arrive at the open
question already knowing which eight ways the attempt goes wrong.

## 6. The reading

Weaknesses are named because a reading list that only recommends is a reading list that produces a
new romance in place of the old one.

### The core five

1. **Thandika Mkandawire** — the single most important name here, and the least likely to be
   encountered by accident. Malawian economist, former director of the UN Research Institute for
   Social Development (UNRISD), later chair in African Development at LSE. He argued **for** developmental welfare states in Africa, against dependency
   fatalism and against the "Africa is uniquely dysfunctional" literature at the same time. Start
   with *Social Policy in a Development Context* (ed., 2004) and the essay "Neopatrimonialism and the
   Political Economy of Economic Performance in Africa: Critical Reflections" (*World Politics*,
   2015). He knew the Nordic model closely and thought parts of it were portable — which makes him
   the exact interlocutor for this question, asked by someone who is not romanticizing either side.
   *Weakness:* his positive program is thinner than his critique; he is better on why the existing
   explanations fail than on what replaces them.

2. **Morten Jerven, *Poor Numbers* (2013)** — the epistemics book, and the one to read second so it
   colors everything after. A Norwegian economic historian showing that much of the African
   statistical record used by *every* camp is unreliable or constructed. Pair with *Africa: Why
   Economists Get It Wrong* (2015). *Weakness:* strong on demolition, offers less on what to do
   instead — though "know your numbers are weak" is itself a discipline.

3. **Walter Rodney, *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* (1972)** — the primary source for the
   dependency frame, in its own voice, brilliantly written. Read it rather than reading about it, so
   the argument can be judged at full strength. *Weakness:* the counterfactual is asserted, not
   demonstrated — what Africa would have become absent Europe is the load-bearing claim and the least
   evidenced one; and it predates fifty years of development economics.

4. **Gurminder Bhambra**, "Relations of extraction, relations of redistribution: Empire, nation, and
   the construction of the British welfare state" (*British Journal of Sociology*, 2022), and
   *Colonialism and Modern Social Theory* (with John Holmwood, 2021). The rigorous version of
   Proposition B: Beveridge's welfare state as fiscally imperial, with a membership defined against
   colonial subjects who were legally British and excluded from its provision. Archival where the
   strong form is theoretical. *Weakness:* Britain is the ideal case for the argument; the thesis
   would be tested harder against Sweden or Finland, and isn't.

5. **Erik Reinert, *How Rich Countries Got Rich... and Why Poor Countries Stay Poor* (2007)** — a
   Norwegian explaining Norway's own ascent as deliberate emulation and industrial policy rather than
   virtue, culture, or homogeneity. Pair with **Ha-Joon Chang, *Kicking Away the Ladder* (2002)**,
   which makes the institutional case that the now-rich used exactly the policies they now forbid —
   and does so without requiring any of the value-theory commitments that make Proposition B's strong
   form contestable. *Weakness:* both under-weight cases where protection produced durable
   rent-seeking rather than capability.

### Track A — extraction, measured carefully

- **Ndikumana & Boyce, *On the Trail of Capital Flight from Africa* (2022)** and *Africa's Odious
  Debts* (2011). The observable-mechanism approach: mis-invoicing, offshore accounts, odious debt.
  The strongest evidence for ongoing extraction precisely because the mechanism is countable. This is
  the book that makes Trap 7 concrete.
- **Sylla & Pigeaud, *Africa's Last Colonial Currency: The CFA Franc Story* (2021)**. The specific,
  still-operating arrangement. Ndongo Samba Sylla is Senegalese and writes from inside the affected
  monetary zone.
- **Jason Hickel, *The Divide* (2017)** and the unequal-exchange papers. Read *after* Jerven, so the
  accounting's dependence on its value theory is visible rather than invisible.
- **Samir Amin** — *Eurocentrism* is the tractable entry; *Accumulation on a World Scale* is the
  machinery. Optional unless Proposition B's strong form needs judging at the source.

### Track B — African agency, against the dependency frame

Read these so the program does not simply replace one confident narrative with another.

- **Claude Ake, *Democracy and Development in Africa* (1996)** — development in Africa did not fail;
  it was never actually attempted. Political conditions over economic determinism.
- **Dambisa Moyo, *Dead Aid* (2009)** — a Zambian economist rejecting the dependency frame outright.
  *Weakness:* the diagnosis is sharper than the prescription, which is fairly conventional market
  policy.
- **Célestin Monga & Justin Yifu Lin, *Beating the Odds* (2017)** — pragmatic structural economics
  aimed at what a poor country can actually do next quarter.

### Track C — the measure-design track (highest direct relevance)

- **Alkire, Foster, Seth, Santos, Roche & Ballon, *Multidimensional Poverty Measurement and Analysis*
  (2015)** — see §5. The technical heart of the program for anyone building the aggregation layer.
- **Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990)** and *Understanding Institutional Diversity*
  (2005) — the diagnostic-not-prescriptive posture, and the design principles.
- **James C. Scott, *Seeing Like a State* (1998)** — Trap 5 at book length. Read it as a warning
  addressed to us specifically.
- **Amartya Sen, *Development as Freedom* (1999)** — reframes what "made it" means. If capability is
  the metric rather than income, every comparison in this document changes shape, including the one
  that started it.
- **Franklin Obeng-Odoom, *The Commons in an Age of Uncertainty* (2021)** and *Property, Institutions,
  and Social Stratification in Africa* (2020) — a Ghanaian political economist working the land-rent
  and commons question from inside African political economy. The least obvious recommendation here
  and possibly the most directly useful, because it is the commons question asked where the
  institutional context is least like the one the commons literature was built in.

### Track D — red-teaming the Nordic model on its own terms

- **Berggren & Trägårdh, *The Swedish Theory of Love: Individualism and Social Trust in Modern
  Sweden* (2022)** — the *statist individualism* thesis: the Nordic state exists to free the
  individual from dependence on family, church, and civil society. If that is right, the Nordic model
  is **individualist infrastructure, not communitarian solidarity** — close to the opposite of a care
  economy grounded in household and community. This is the most uncomfortable book on the list for
  anyone building in this direction, and therefore the most necessary.
- **Esping-Andersen, *The Three Worlds of Welfare Capitalism* (1990)** — the taxonomy and the
  vocabulary (decommodification, the three regimes). Necessary for precision.
- **Broberg & Roll-Hansen, *Eugenics and the Welfare State* (1996)** — the documentary basis for
  Proposition A across Denmark, Sweden, Norway, and Finland.
- **Michael Ross, *The Oil Curse* (2012)** — Trap 1 at book length, and the standard account of why
  Norway is the exception.
- **Korpi & Palme**, "The Paradox of Redistribution and Strategies of Equality" (*American
  Sociological Review*, 1998) — Trap 2 in its original statement. A paper, not a book; read it in an
  afternoon.

## 7. The standing constraint

The outcome this work is for is an emergent middle class — meaning broadly distributed economic
security and standing, not a boundary between classes — and an emergent trust, both arising
**from** the protocol's care economy rather than being legislated onto it. In the document's own
terms: Mechanism 7's associational substrate and Mechanism 3's universalist coalition, grown from
the bottom — held under Trap 2's warning that the moment either is measured as a class of
beneficiaries, the measure manufactures the fragility it names. The constraint is that they must
arise without importing the traps of the context they arise in — and each context's traps are different, intimately local, and known best by the people
living inside them.

Texas is not Alaska: same resource, different institutional sequence, different available policy.
Kenya is not Guatemala: different colonial arrangement, different associational substrate, different
statistical legibility. Norway is not a template; it is one path whose preconditions were mostly
accidental and partly indefensible.

The generalization the whole program supports is narrow and worth stating exactly:

> **The protocol's job is not to know which policy is right. It is to make the local context legible
> enough, and its own priors visible enough, that a council composed of people who live there can
> tell the difference between a structural fact and an artifact of the measure.**

Every trap in §4 is a way that job fails while appearing to succeed. That is why the reading comes
before the design, and why the traps are the deliverable rather than the conclusions.
