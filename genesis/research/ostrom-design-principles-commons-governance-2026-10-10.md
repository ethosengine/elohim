# Ostrom's design principles — the primary-source pass

*Owed twice. The commons-data-pools capture graded its Ostrom row "⚠ secondary summary; a primary-source pass is owed before the checklist is canon" ([§6](epr:commons-data-pools-hot-path-and-external-tooling-2026-09-11)), and the search research carried the same mark: "⚠ unverified citation to Ostrom 1990; worth a primary-source pass" ([§4](epr:search-discovery-incumbent-power-and-p2p-inversion-2026-09-11)). The WeAll review then leaned on Ostrom by name for its small-N answer. This document pays the debt: it reads the principles in Ostrom's own last wording, reads the evidence record and the critiques, reads the three places the principles were carried into digital commons, and then walks all eleven against the tree at `file:line`. §6, added 2026-10-11, is what the elohim absorbs and what the person sees, the place this design departs from Ostrom's; §7 is the checklist a pool must pass; §6 and §7 together are the body of the `elohim-commons-design` skill (§10).*

**Verification key:** ✅ read in the primary text this pass · ◐ single secondary source, or a fetched summary of a paper not read in full · ⚠ web summary only, or contradicted.

**Terms, once.** A *Collective* is our notarized group record (charter, founder, members); a *Membership* is a member's own signed row in it, with a withdrawal height; a *Commitment* is a signed governance record in the Resource-Event-Agent (REA) vocabulary; a *Mishpat lens* is an authored rule with a declared role (`lens`, `floor`, `ceiling`) and a telos; *reach* is the eight-level scope ladder on every record and *standing* is a person's earned, relational track record of trust; a *holon* is a group whole in itself and part of a larger one; a *sortition council* is a randomly drawn, term-limited council of ordinary people that can overturn an elohim; a *pool* is a Collective that holds a `commons-pool:<kind>` resource and is party to its members' contribution commitments ([spec](epr:commons-pool-as-collective-party-design)).

**Sovereignty frame (bounded).** Ostrom's own vocabulary is "self-governance" and "self-organization", and her critics' sharpest line is Agrawal's: the field "loosen[s] assumptions about sovereign selves and apolitical property rights institutions" too rarely (Agrawal 2003:244 ✅). We quote both. Our ontology already refuses the sovereign self at the apex (Stance IV.1): standing is inherent and backstopped by community, never asserted by a key. Ostrom's cases are on our side of that line, which is the point of §5(a): every one of her enduring commons is a *community* holding a resource under its own rules, not a set of self-sovereign individuals. Our canon's "Nested Sovereignty" (governance-layers §2) is her principle 8 under another name, and it is read here as nested *jurisdiction*, which is what she means.

**Method.** One orchestrator, no sub-agents. Read in full this pass: Ostrom's Nobel lecture (the Indiana Workshop draft of 2009-12-08, headed "DRAFT — DO NOT QUOTE"; the published text is *American Economic Review* 100(3):641–672, 2010, and quotations below are from the draft and should be re-checked against the published page before any external use) ✅; Cox, Arnold & Villamayor-Tomás, "A Review of Design Principles for Community-based Natural Resource Management", *Ecology and Society* 15(4):38, 2010 ✅; Cox, Arnold & Villamayor-Tomás, "Design Principles are not Blue Prints, but are They Robust? A Meta-analysis of 112 Studies", Lincoln Institute working paper, 2009 ✅; Wilson, Ostrom & Cox, "Generalizing the core design principles for the efficacy of groups", *JEBO* 90S:S21–S32, 2013 ✅; Hess & Ostrom, "A Framework for Analyzing the Knowledge Commons", draft 12-2005 of chapter 3 of *Understanding Knowledge as a Commons* (MIT Press 2007), from the Digital Library of the Commons ✅; Agrawal, "Sustainable Governance of Common-Pool Resources: Context, Methods, and Politics", *Annual Review of Anthropology* 32:243–262, 2003 ✅; Rozas, Tenorio-Fornés, Díaz-Molina & Hassan, "When Ostrom Meets Blockchain", *SAGE Open* 11(1), 2021 ✅; Forte & Bruckman, "Scaling Consensus: Increasing Decentralization in Wikipedia Governance", Georgia Tech, from the Digital Library of the Commons ✅. Read as fetched summaries only: Baggio et al. 2016 (*IJC* 10(2)) ◐; Esposito, Tse & Goh 2025 (*Frontiers in Blockchain* 8) ◐; the open-data-ecosystems paper (arXiv 2208.01694) ◐; the Governing Knowledge Commons framework via its author's own summary ⚠. **Not read:** *Governing the Commons* (1990) itself. Its Table 3.1 is not online; the wording used here is the Cox 2010 reformulation that Ostrom herself adopted in the Nobel lecture, which is the better citation for current use. All PDFs were handled as untrusted downloads in an isolated scratch directory and text-extracted; nothing was executed from them.

## 1. The principles, in the wording Ostrom adopted last

She was explicit about what they are. "I used the term 'design principle' to characterize these regularities. I did not mean that the fishers, irrigators, pastoralists, and others overtly had these principles in their minds when they developed systems that survived for long periods of time. My effort was to identify a set of core underlying lessons that characterized the long sustained regimes as contrasted to the cases of failure" (Nobel draft p.13 ✅). And in the footnote: "The term 'design principle' has confused many readers. Perhaps I should have used the term 'best practices' to describe the rules and structure of robust institutions" (n.5 ✅). She then listed "a brief updated list as developed by Cox, Arnold, and Villamayor-Tomás (2009)" and said "I have adopted the improvements to principles 1, 2, and 4" ✅. That list, verbatim:

| # | Principle (Ostrom 2009, adopting Cox et al.) |
|---|---|
| **1A** | *User Boundaries:* Clear and locally understood boundaries between legitimate users and nonusers are present. |
| **1B** | *Resource Boundaries:* Clear boundaries that separate a specific common-pool resource from a larger social-ecological system are present. |
| **2A** | *Congruence with Local Conditions:* Appropriation and provision rules are congruent with local social and environmental conditions. |
| **2B** | *Appropriation and Provision:* Appropriation rules are congruent with provision rules; the distribution of costs is proportional to the distribution of benefits. |
| **3** | *Collective-Choice Arrangements:* Most individuals affected by a resource regime are authorized to participate in making and modifying its rules. |
| **4A** | *Monitoring Users:* Individuals who are accountable to or are the users monitor the appropriation and provision levels of the users. |
| **4B** | *Monitoring the Resource:* Individuals who are accountable to or are the users monitor the condition of the resource. |
| **5** | *Graduated Sanctions:* Sanctions for rule violations start very low but become stronger if a user repeatedly violates a rule. |
| **6** | *Conflict-Resolution Mechanisms:* Rapid, low-cost, local arenas exist for resolving conflicts among users or with officials. |
| **7** | *Minimal Recognition of Rights:* The rights of local users to make their own rules are recognized by the government. |
| **8** | *Nested Enterprises:* When a common-pool resource is closely connected to a larger social-ecological system, governance activities are organized in multiple nested layers. |

Cox's own Table 4 wording differs in two places worth keeping (Cox 2010 ✅): 2B reads "The benefits obtained by users from a common-pool resource (CPR), as determined by appropriation rules, are proportional to the amount of inputs required in the form of labor, material, or money, as determined by provision rules"; and 8 enumerates the activities: "Appropriation, provision, monitoring, enforcement, conflict resolution, and governance activities are organized in multiple layers of nested enterprises."

**The 2013 generalization** (Wilson, Ostrom & Cox ✅) restates the eight for any group "whose members are not close relatives, proven friends, or coping with a dire emergency" and changes two headings. Principle 2 becomes **"Proportional equivalence between benefits and costs.** Members of the group must negotiate a system that rewards members for their contributions. High status or other disproportionate benefits must be earned. Unfair inequality poisons collective efforts." Principle 8 becomes "For groups that are part of larger social systems, there must be appropriate coordination among relevant groups. Every sphere of activity has an optimal scale... a concept called polycentric governance... A related concept is subsidiarity, which assigns governance tasks by default to the lowest jurisdiction, unless this is explicitly determined to be ineffective." Principle 3 gains the line "People hate being told what to do but will work hard for group goals that they have agreed upon," and 5 the line "Often gossip or a gentle reminder is sufficient, but more severe forms of punishment must also be waiting in the wings."

## 2. What the evidence says the principles are, and are not

**They hold, as a set, and the sharpest criticism was theoretical.** Cox 2010 coded 91 studies ✅: the overall mean evaluation was 3.73 on a five-point scale, "slightly below the value for moderately supportive"; studies that were abstract rather than empirical had a median of 2.0, and "much of the criticism or lack of support directed at the principles" came from them. Across 77 codable cases "every one had at least twice as many supportive cases as unsupportive." By principle: 2A and 2B "very strong"; 4B "very strongly supported"; 1A "strong"; 1B and 4A "moderate"; 3, 5, 6, 7 and 8 "moderately well supported"; principle 8 the weakest statistically (significant only at 10%). Principle 1 was "the most frequent target of (primarily theoretical) criticisms", the complaint being that "it is too rigid and that, in many systems, fuzzier social boundaries" work. The 2009 meta-analysis of 112 studies reached the same place ✅.

**They are configural, not a checklist of independent items.** Baggio et al. 2016 recoded 69 of Cox's cases with qualitative comparative analysis ◐: no single principle is necessary and sufficient; successful cases averaged about 8.7 principles present against 4.3 in failures; every case with 10 or 11 present succeeded; congruence (2A, 2B) is "the linchpin for success"; and the *absence* of 2A, 2B, 4B or 5 "strongly raised the odds of failure." Which principles matter most shifts with the resource: social boundaries (1A) for mobile resources, monitoring (4) for static ones. Their first conclusion: "DPs should not be viewed in isolation."

**They are incomplete, and the missing part is named.** Cox calls incompleteness "the most important empirical critique we found in the literature" ✅ and quotes Harkes (2006) from Indonesian *sasi* fisheries: "the real 'glue' that keeps an institution alive over time are the social mechanisms, i.e., trust, legitimacy, and transparency," and Singleton & Taylor (1992): the deeper feature of the successful systems is "a community of mutually vulnerable actors." Agrawal's 2003 review is the fuller version ✅: "scholars of commons have discovered far more variables that potentially affect resource management than can be analyzed carefully." His Table 1 synthesizes Wade, Ostrom, and Baland & Platteau into some thirty facilitating conditions beyond the eight, among them: small group size; shared norms; past successful experience (social capital); "heterogeneity of endowments, homogeneity of identities and interests"; interdependence among members; overlap between where users live and where the resource is; high dependence on the resource; rules that are "simple and easy to understand"; "low-cost exclusion technology"; and "supportive external sanctioning institutions." Several of these are things a substrate can supply and the principles cannot (§5b).

**What they are for.** The 2009 meta-analysis states the function in one sentence ✅: "The primary role of the design principles is to explain under what conditions trust and reciprocity can be built and maintained in order to sustain collective action in the face of collective action problems posed by common-pool resources." The model of the person they presuppose is Ostrom's "fallible, norm-adopting individuals who pursue contingent strategies in complex, uncertain environments" (1990:185, quoted ✅). The Nobel lecture puts the layer beneath the principles into six **microsituational variables** that raise cooperation in every experiment ✅: (1) "Communication is feasible with the full set of participants"; (2) "Reputations of participants are known"; (3) "High marginal per capita return"; (4) "Entry or exit capabilities"; (5) "Longer time horizon"; (6) "Agreed-upon sanctioning capabilities. While external sanctions or imposed sanctioning systems may reduce cooperation, when participants themselves agree to a sanctioning system they frequently do not need to use sanctions at a high volume." The summary line: people "are more likely to cooperate when situational variables increase the likelihood of gaining trust that others will reciprocate" ✅.

**They are not a blueprint.** Cox answers the Blaikie/Cleaver/Scott "Trojan horse" charge directly ✅: "Each design principle stipulates a range of conditions that could satisfy it, rather than a particular rule," and "the dichotomy between diagnostic and design principle approaches is false." Wilson, Ostrom & Cox give the mechanism ✅: a principle is ultimate causation, an implementation is proximate; "If there are 10 successful ways to monitor (for example), then the correlation between any one way and group performance will be weak. Only when she combined different implementations into functional categories was she able to demonstrate a strong association." Hence "the design principles cannot be implemented in a cookie cutter fashion but require a process of local adaptation to find the best implementations," and every group also needs **auxiliary principles** for its particular objective. Ostrom's own last word on policy: "a core goal of public policy should be to facilitate the development of institutions that bring out the best in humans" ✅.

**Hardin's error, in her words.** "Many presumed that all common-pool resources were owned by no one. Thus, it was thought that government officials had to impose new external variables" ✅. The enduring commons were never open access; they were *common property*, held by a bounded community with its own rules. §5(a) takes that up against our own phrase.

## 3. Where the principles bend for a digital commons

**Hess & Ostrom (2005/2007) did not carry the eight principles across.** The knowledge-commons framework chapter contains no discussion of the design principles at all ✅ (absence verified in the draft text). What it carries instead is the IAD triad (biophysical characteristics, attributes of the community, rules-in-use) and three distinctions a pool here needs:

- The resource is threefold: **facilities, artifacts, ideas** ✅. "Facilities store artifacts and make them available... The physical network infrastructure includes the optical fiber, copper wire switches, routers, host computers, and end-user workstations... It also includes the amount of bandwidth." Artifacts are "the physical resource or flow units of a facility." Ideas are "a pure public good and, therefore nonrivalrous." Suber's line, quoted by them: the open-access commons "is non-rivalrous because it is digital, not because it is OA."
- The bundle of rights is seven, not one ✅: **access, contribution, extraction, removal, management/participation, exclusion, alienation**. "Removal: the right to remove one's artifacts from the resource." "Alienation: the right to sell or lease management and exclusion rights."
- The dilemmas are different ✅: enclosure, pollution, degradation, inequity, *underuse*, noncompliance, withdrawal. And the free-rider is redefined: "Others outside that community who browse, search, read, download, or print out documents in the repository are not free riding. In fact, they enhance the quality of the resource by using it." Free riding "can only occur with those members of the local knowledge commons... who are expected to contribute."

The Governing Knowledge Commons school (Madison, Frischmann, Strandburg) goes further and says Ostrom's design principles "have no definitive application to knowledge commons" without empirical work, because "knowledge resources are, in principle, abundant rather than scarce" ⚠.

**Three groups did carry them across, and found the same bend each time.** Forte & Bruckman on Wikipedia ✅: "like many natural resources, namespace is consumable; only one article exists on any given topic," and "defining membership boundaries... does not have the same meaning since millions of people outside the community benefit directly." Their one gap, from eleven interviews: "the inability of WikiProject members to enforce their own local guidelines in order to maintain local standards" — principle 7 failing *inside* the commons, the parent not recognizing the child's rules. The open-data-ecosystems paper ◐: the subtractable element of a digital commons is "the resources required to maintain the quality and value of the data, rather than the availability of data," so principles 1 and 3 carry unchanged, 6, 7 and 8 are modified for a global commons with no global jurisdiction, and 2, 4 and 5 are "conceptually different" because a platform provider holds the central monitoring and sanction tools, "which shifts power." Rozas et al. on blockchain ✅: six affordances (tokenization; self-enforcement and formalization of rules; autonomous automatization; decentralization of power over infrastructure; increasing transparency; codification of trust), each mapped to the principles it can serve, with two cautions that bind us. First, "rules embedded in smart contracts rely on an ex-ante nature, rather than ex-post," so a sanction in code cannot "start very low" and read the context of the offense. Second, and against our instinct: "counterintuitively... the changes experienced in the self-organizational processes of CBPP communities tend to show an increase in the degree of formalization around decision-making over time when they grow, which is explained as a means to achieve decentralization and to scale up" (citing Forte, Schweik & English, Rozas & Huckle) — "even in cases with a generally anti-bureaucratic attitude." Their open question is ours: "Which aspects should remain in/off the blockchain, or further completely in/out of code?" The 2025 DAO case study ◐ answers for token voting: "This consolidation of governing power creates incentives for collusion and vote-buying"; delegation "concentrates power among delegates"; the paper's headline is "centralized control disguised as decentralization."

**What this means for a pool here.** A pool is two commons on one object, and Hess & Ostrom's threefold split tells them apart. Its *facility* (members' storage bytes, compute minutes, bandwidth, and the labor of keeping an index fresh) is a true common-pool resource: rival, congestible, depletable per period. Its *artifacts and ideas* (the tiles, the index, the model, the records) are a knowledge commons: non-rival, enclosable, pollutable, and better the more they are used. The tree already names the first class: `Rivalry::PartiallyRival` ("Congestible — degrades under heavy use (bandwidth, shared compute)"), `Excludability::ConditionallyExcludable` ("Excludable within governance structure (commons with Ostrom rules)"), and `GoodsClassification::CommonPool` with `requires_consent_allocation()` true for it (`elohim-storage/src/services/resource_nature.rs:50-66, :157-166, :197-200` ✅). So the eight principles govern the facility and the contribution labor; the knowledge-commons bundle of rights governs the artifacts; and the checklist in §7 asks both.

## 5. Challenges to our own assumptions

**(a) "Owned by no one" is Hardin's phrase, not Ostrom's.** Values First says the honest commons test is "owned by no one, non-extractive, and structurally unenclosable" (`values-forward.md:64` ✅) and that Stance I.2 shows our substrate passes it. Ostrom's lecture: the tragedy followed from presuming "all common-pool resources were owned by no one" ✅. Her enduring cases are owned, by a bounded community, under rules the community can change; what no one holds is the right to sell it. Hess & Ostrom's bundle makes the sentence precise: a commons is a resource whose members hold **access, contribution, extraction, removal, management and exclusion**, and over which **alienation** is held by no one. A pool with exclusion (the reach gate) and no alienation is a commons; a pool with no exclusion is open access and Hardin applies to it. Candidate one-line amendment for the operator, not applied here: *"held by its members, alienable by no one."* The canon's stronger claim, that the substrate makes enclosure *structurally* impossible, is a claim about alienation and should say so.

**(b) The substrate supplies the conditions the principles cannot, and fails one of them.** Agrawal's thirty conditions include several the protocol delivers by construction: reputations known (micro-var 2: standing is a relational record); long time horizon (micro-var 5: records persist, reach is earned over time); low-cost exit (micro-var 4: withdrawal by one update); "low-cost exclusion technology" (the reach gate); interdependence (the holder sees the draw). The one it cannot deliver is micro-var 1, **"communication is feasible with the full set of participants."** A pool of a thousand members fails it on the day it opens. Ostrom's answer is principle 8, and Wilson–Ostrom–Cox's is "every sphere of activity has an optimal scale." So a design rule follows that none of our pool documents states: **a pool is sized to the communication condition, or it nests.** The household pool (gate decision 4) satisfies it trivially; a planetary tile pool satisfies it only as a nest of pools, each small enough to talk.

**(c) Gradient over closure runs against what every scaling digital commons did.** The WeAll review's bet was "they buy closure and pay in flatness; we buy gradient and pay in closure." Rozas, Forte, and Schweik & English report that growing commons *formalize* their decision-making, "counterintuitively," as the means to decentralize ✅. Row 32's closure artifact is therefore not a concession to a chain; it is what Ostrom's digital descendants observed every commons doing on the way to scale. Hold the gradient; build the artifact.

**(d) Monitoring is of appropriation, never of persons.** Principle 4 watches "appropriation and provision levels" and "the condition of the resource." Our 4A instrument is a draw ledger the holder can see, which is Ostrom's form exactly and costs the norm-abiding member nothing. The Observer Protocol is a different instrument for a different harm and must not be read as pool monitoring; a pool that watches its members instead of its draws has swapped Ostrom for surveillance, and the open-data paper's warning (the platform holding the monitoring and sanction tools "shifts power") lands on us if the pool's elohim becomes that platform.

**(e) No tokens, and now the field says why.** Our capture already recorded that "every token-incentivized commons project surveyed failed while the governance-first ones endured" (README §Discovery ✅). Rozas's tokenization affordance comes with the risk that pooled value is re-read as a market; the 2025 DAO study finds token-weighted voting "creates incentives for collusion and vote-buying" ◐. A pool's contribution record is an REA event with a declared reach, never a transferable unit, and that is now a sourced refusal rather than an instinct.

**(f) A principle is not a mechanism, so the skill must not prescribe one.** "If there are 10 successful ways to monitor... the correlation between any one way and group performance will be weak." A checklist that demands *our* monitoring implementation fails Ostrom's own method. §7 asks for the function and the declaration, and names the instrument in the tree as one way, not the way.

**(g) The sortition floor is the WeAll lesson restated by Ostrom.** "When participants themselves agree to a sanctioning system they frequently do not need to use sanctions at a high volume" (micro-var 6 ✅). A council drawn to the reach of the decision is the participants agreeing; a sanction imposed by an elohim reading a constant is the "externally imposed" case that "may reduce cooperation." The WeAll parameter-ordering row (commons row 33) and this finding want the same artifact.

## 6. The elohim's share — what it absorbs, and what the person sees

*Added 2026-10-11, after the operator asked what the elohim's place in the architecture does to Ostrom's design: "what elohim will absorb specifically vs what we surface to humans in the design and UI." Read against canon only; no new external source. The earlier sections stand; this one changes how §7 is read and what §8 takes.*

**Ostrom's principles are a theory of where governance labor sits when intelligence is expensive.** Each principle keeps one cost low enough for ordinary members to bear: knowing who is in (1), fitting and reading the rules (2), deliberating (3), watching (4), sanctioning without error (5), settling (6), defending the right to do any of this (7), and talking at scale (8). Her enduring commons were small because every one of those costs was paid in human attention. That is why small group size sits among Agrawal's thirty conditions, why micro-variable 1 is communication with the full set, and why the strong form of principle 4 is monitors who *are* the appropriators: watching was a byproduct of working, so it cost nothing extra. Cox's reading that the principles' "primary role" is "to explain under what conditions trust and reciprocity can be built and maintained" says the same from the other side: trust is what lets a group stop paying those costs on every interaction. Our own plan puts it plainly: "A functioning commons does not re-investigate its participants every time they cooperate" (`ai-stewarded-commons-reimplementation-plan.md:372` ✅).

The canon's reframe is a claim about exactly those costs. "Moderation is centralized because intelligence used to be expensive"; with an elohim at every node, wisdom gates at authoring, relay and consumption, and the substrate "returns to coordination tooling that wisdom *uses*" (`ubiquitous-wisdom-dissolves-chokepoint.md:42-54` ✅). Read against Ostrom, the three gates are where principles 1, 2, 4 and the first rung of 5 are paid, and the payer moves from the member's attention to the member's own agent. That is the different approach the operator named: **the field minimized governance labor by keeping groups small; this design absorbs it at every member's endpoint.** But the same canon fixes what the agent may absorb. It is "a high-variety attenuator on the person's behalf, not a container for the person" (`observability/epic.md:460` ✅). "No machine here will ever hold final authority over you that a human floor cannot overturn" (`values-forward.md:165` ✅). "An agent that *sets* credit limits is a central planner with better manners"; one that computes, shows, flags and proposes "is a clerk" (`succession.md:270` ✅). Model output never supplies its own permission, and "a retry does not mint fresh authority" (plan §5.2, `:217-219` ✅). "The person is never the operator... The person is asked for assent or told an outcome with its evidence" (`observability/epic.md:447-450` ✅). And authority is vested in the diversity of the network and the validation of claims, never in a device or an agent; the elohim refuses client authority, and the elohim ceiling is plural, witnessed, challengeable adjudication, not a seat ([WeAll review](epr:weall-protocol-peer-review-2026-10-10), the 2026-10-10 correction ✅). So the split is not "the AI governs the pool." It is:

> **The elohim absorbs the variety of sensing, reading, carrying, matching, drafting and the first rung of consequence, inside standing mandates, and it does so for its own person. The person holds every act that makes a rule, changes a rule, admits or excludes, sanctions beyond notice, settles, recognizes, or stakes their standing.** The elohim's output is a proposal with evidence or an outcome with evidence, never a verdict. And there is no pool-elohim, because a pool is a party (a `Collective`), not a person.

**In Beer's terms.** The project already reads itself through the Viable System Model (`shefa.md:63`, `observability/epic.md:452-461` ✅). A pool is a viable system. Its members with their elohim are System 1. The shared lens, with the elohim's de-duplication, loop suppression and debounce, is System 2 (plan §5.4 `:266`, §6.4 `:460` ✅). The proportionality bargain is System 3 and the draw-ledger reading is System 3*, the sporadic direct inspection that checks whether the reports are true. The pool-condition trend and the pool's relation to the pools beside and above it are System 4. The charter, who may join, and what the pool is for are System 5. The social-reach back-propagation is the algedonic channel, pain carried hop by hop, and "the first receiver of every signal is the person who felt it" (`observability/epic.md:458-459` ✅). **The elohim absorbs Systems 2 and 3*, the computation inside 3, the sensing in 4, and the carriage of the algedonic signal. The members hold System 5, the bargain in 3, and every algedonic response beyond the first rung.** Requisite variety is met on the resource side by the elohim and on the policy side by the people, and the policy side is small enough for people to hold at any scale. That is why ubiquitous wisdom raises Ostrom's ceiling on *coordination* and not on *choice*: "every mediation attenuates" (`:456-457`), so an assent carried through an elohim is not communication with the full set, and §5(b)'s size-or-nest rule binds the *deciding* body while the elohim carries coordination at any size.

**The three gates, against the principles.**

| Gate | What the elohim does there | Principles paid there |
|---|---|---|
| **Authoring**: a contribution or a draw is about to leave the person | Checks the act against the pool's holdings and lens and against its own person's carrying balance. If the act would cross a rule it says so *before the door closes* ("want to add context, narrow who sees it, or share with awareness?") and records that the person was told and chose (`social_medium/epic.md:52, :124-130` ✅). | 1B, 2A, 2B, and rung one of 5 |
| **Relay**: a hop decides whether to carry | Decides to carry against the recipient's contracts, the trail and the lens. The carry is a witnessed provision event on the holder's side. Feedback, corrections and notices travel back along the same path, one hop at a time (`social-reach-nervous-system.md:36-50` ✅; plan §5.4 `:243-290`). | 4A by construction, and the carriage for 6 |
| **Consumption**: what surfaces for the person | Verifies membership. Surfaces the pool's condition as care proportional to a declared bound, never as a series to read. Surfaces one concrete choice when a decision is genuinely the person's (plan §1 interface contract, `:101-113` ✅). | 1A, 4B, and the human half of 3, 5 and 6 |

**Beneath the eleven, one condition our bend adds.** Ostrom's presupposed conditions were communication, known reputations and agreed sanctioning. Vesting authority in "the diversity of the network" adds a fourth: **plurality of judgment.** If every member's elohim is the same model under the same prompt, the distributed monitor is one judgment at every node, and the chokepoint has been rebuilt as a monoculture. The graduation evaluator already declares a *diversity threshold* per attestation kind (`observability/epic.md:440-441` ✅); nothing declares one for a pool's closure. A pool should declare how many distinct elohim lineages hold its members' seats, and what agreement across them a binding outcome needs. (A first draft of this paragraph added a second leg, concentration of members behind one hosting doorway. The operator struck it on 2026-10-11: hosting is the transitional phase of the stewardship ladder, not a standing condition; doorways are a thin web2 federation, DNS, TLS and caching, whose conductor costs make hosting unattractive and flywheel people into stewardship; and a person on the authoritative p2p dataplane may register many doorways for web2 ingress. A doorway is not a monitor and its count is not a plurality measure. The lineage leg stands alone.) Esposito's token-collusion finding (§2) is this condition in a chain's vocabulary.

**What changes from off-the-shelf Ostrom.**

1. **Monitoring is not a cost to minimize by staying small.** It is a byproduct of each member's elohim at the gates, in Ostrom's strong form, with no monitor role to capture. The checklist must therefore refuse a monitor role, not design one.
2. **The first rung of consequence moves before the act.** Ostrom's sanctions were after the fact. Here "the primary vehicle of accountability is not the network's response after the fact. It is Maria's elohim, sitting beside her at the moment she is about to share" (`social_medium/epic.md:124`). Most violations become conversations that end before the act, which is Stance IV.2 made cheap.
3. **Collective choice becomes cheap to convene and impossible to delegate.** The elohim condenses views and computes closure, so amendments can be frequent and small; the one act it never touches is the vote. Row 35's closure artifact must be deterministic for that reason.
4. **Nesting is relieved for coordination and kept for choice.**
5. **Resource monitoring is never a dashboard.** The UI deliverable for 4B is a bound, a care notice, and a choice.

**Challenges to our own bend.**

- **(i) Absorbed variety is invisible variety.** If the elohim prevents most violations at the authoring gate, members never see the rules working, and legitimacy, Harkes's "glue" (§2), thins; rules nobody sees applied become rules nobody can amend wisely. The interface contract's answer is "intelligible on demand," and a pool should carry one aggregate its members can read: notices per period, never who.
- **(ii) Consumption shaping is not pool policy.** The consumption gate shapes what surfaces "against context, standing, and care" (`ubiquitous-wisdom:48`). A pool's lens governs draws. It must not reach into a member's consumption shaping or preference guards (`social-reach-nervous-system.md:75-99`), or the pool is watching persons through their own agents.
- **(iii) Counsel is to the person, not to the act.** An elohim that keeps defending its person against their own request to stop (`constitution.md:186`) is the right shape for a member under a coordinated campaign and the wrong shape if it ever defends a draw its person did not choose; the authoring-gate record of "told and chose" is what keeps the two apart.
- **(iv) Hosting is a stage, not a seat.** A member in the hosted phase of the stewardship ladder is still one member with one elohim; the doorway that fronts them is a thin web2 federation, plural and registrable by anyone on the dataplane, and it holds no monitor's seat in a pool. Do not read "hosted" as "represented by the host." The relation runs the other way: a doorway is subject to the commons governance of those who ingress through it (operator, 2026-10-11). Its capacity (names, bandwidth, the conductor minutes of its hosted rung) is a facility in Hess & Ostrom's sense, rival and depletable per period, and the people who ingress are its appropriators and provisioners. A doorway is therefore a *subject* of §7, one of the first pools the checklist should be run against, and never a party that governs its users.
- **(v) The seat is born narrow.** The elohim-operator's own rule (the 2026-10-09 wedged-peer design) applies to any steward seat a pool names: it may Permit alone only what the charter names, and Refers everything else to the members.

## 7. The pool checklist — what a commons pool here must declare

Eleven questions, asked of one pool at a time, in the order the evidence weights them (congruence first, nesting last). Each names the function Ostrom's principle serves, the instrument in the tree as *one* implementation, the declaration a pool's charter must carry, and the probe that would show the principle present. A pool passes a question only when the declaration exists *and* the probe can run; a declaration with no probe is `unwired`, in the habit register's sense.

| Q | Principle | Function it serves | Instrument in tree (one way) | The pool declares | Probe |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2A congruence | Rules fit this pool's people and resource, not a template | `author-lens` Commitment, role `floor`/`ceiling`; per-holon ceiling | Its own lens: appropriation rule, provision rule, telos | The lens CID is in the charter and resolves |
| 2 | 2B proportionality | What a member may draw is proportional to what they provide | Holder-side carrying record (gate decision 3); constitutional band | The proportionality rule, and the window it is read over | Two members with 2:1 contribution and a 1:2 draw: the draw check refuses or narrows the second |
| 3 | 4B resource monitoring | Members can see the condition of the resource | `IndexMeasure`; recipe CID; unreadable count | One measure: fill, freshness, unreadables, replica independence | The measure renders for a member without a steward's help |
| 4 | 5 graduated sanctions | A first violation meets a notice; repetition meets more; return is always open | Stance IV.2; reach narrowing on standing | A ladder with at least three rungs, notice first, authored as a lens the members agreed to | A simulated second violation lands on rung two, never rung three |
| 5 | 4A user monitoring | Members can see who drew what | Draw record visible to the holder; public compute draw | Who can list draws, at what reach | A member lists the last N draws without a steward |
| 6 | 1A user boundaries | Who is in, who is out, how one leaves | `Membership` + withdrawal height; the current-membership test | Join rule (sponsor? waiting period?), leave rule | A withdrawn member's draw is refused at step 3 |
| 7 | 1B resource boundaries | What the pool holds and what it does not | `commons-pool:<kind>` resource; recipe CID; arc ranges | The holdings: kinds, arcs or ranges, recipes | A draw outside the declared holdings is refused naming the boundary |
| 8 | 3 collective choice | Members can change the rules | (none: no charter update path) | How a rule changes: who proposes, who decides, at what reach, by what closure artifact | A member-proposed lens change lands, and two peers compute the same outcome |
| 9 | 6 conflict resolution | A fast, cheap, local arena, and an appeal upward | `StewardshipAppeal`; mishpat `Challenge`; co-steward mediation | The arena, its reach, and where appeal goes | A challenge is opened and closed inside the pool's reach, witnessed |
| 10 | 7 recognition | The pool's rules bind at its reach and the parent holon says so | `CollabAgreement` with counter-attestation; row 32 | Which parent recognizes the charter, and what it reserves | The parent's attestation of the charter resolves |
| 11 | 8 nesting / scale | The deciding body is small enough to talk, or it nests; the elohim carries coordination at any size | Reach ladder; sortition council drawn to reach | Its reach, its member ceiling, and the pool above it | Member count ≤ declared ceiling, or a parent pool is named |

**Beneath the eleven, three conditions the principles presuppose** (Nobel lecture, Agrawal): *communication* with the full set of participants (Q11 is its proxy); *reputation* known (standing; supplied); *agreed sanctioning* (Q4's "the members agreed to" is not decoration).

**Where a digital pool bends** (§3): declare the facility and the artifacts separately, because the eight govern the first and the bundle of rights governs the second; name which of the seven rights members hold and state that alienation is held by no one; an outsider who reads is not a free rider; the subtractable resource is capacity and maintenance labor, so provision rules are about those; expect to formalize as you grow.

**Reading the result** (Baggio): count the questions answered with both declaration and probe. Fewer than eight is a pool that the field says will not last; a missing Q1, Q2, Q3 or Q4 is a pool the field says will fail. Report the count and the named gaps, never a score.

**Not open to the checklist**: the implementation. Each "instrument in tree" is one of Ostrom's "10 successful ways"; a pool that satisfies the function another way passes.

**Where the labor sits** is §6. A design that puts any act from §6's "guard" column on the elohim (a vote, an admission, a rung past notice, a settlement, a recognition, a lens in force) fails that question whatever the charter declares, and the finding is written as a chain gap, not a note.
