Responsible Tech Guide 2025

Human Agency

Human Agency is at the heart of shaping our technological future. While technology often feels like an unstoppable force, it is ultimately designed, deployed, and governed by people. Our choices, values, and institutions determine its trajectory.

The name of our organization, All Tech Is Human, signifies our deep belief in Human Agency. This stands in contrast to a purely deterministic view where technology evolves on its own, independent of human direction. By exercising collective responsibility, we can steer tech toward futures that align with our values and is aligned with societal wellbeing. This happens through design, regulation, advocacy, and talent pipelines, and more. Agency puts humans at the center of decision-making in creating a future we want to live in.

Tech Determinism

Technological Determinism is the concept that technology develops according to its own logic and then drives social, cultural, and political change. Change, therefore, is often portrayed as if it's outside of human control. This stands in direct contrast with Agency.

For strong believers of Tech Determinism, after a technology exists we are on an inevitable path. Contrasted with Human Agency, Tech Determinism is focused on technology shaping society, as opposed to society actively shaping technology. All Tech Is Human, by its very name, rejects Tech Determinism and views the concept as a useful worldview to reduce responsibility.

Welcome to the Responsible Tech Movement

There are two prevailing media narratives around our tech future: one views technology as a panacea for all of society's problems, and the other views technology as the key driver creating society's problems. All Tech Is Human sits in the less salacious middle, recognizing that humans are central to driving our future.

The Responsible Tech movement recognizes the significance of technology's impact in shaping us on a personal and societal level, but also our right and responsibility to actively shape technology to fit our interests. The question is never whether technology will influence society, but how, and who gets to decide. I deeply believe in the power of our Agency, which is why I started All Tech Is Human in 2018. It's right there in the name.

The Responsible Tech Guide exists because so many of us, no matter our backgrounds, share the same aspiration: to build a future where technology aligns with our values, strengthens our democracy, and supports human flourishing. But entering this space can feel overwhelming. The field is broad, the language is varied, and the pathways aren't always clear. This confusion reduces the broad participation that the ecosystem needs.

Our guide is meant to serve as a helpful roadmap and invitation. It's a roadmap for those seeking to find their place within Responsible Tech—whether as a student, policymaker, designer, technologist, academic, or advocate. And it's an invitation to join a growing global community that believes technology should be designed and governed with intention, inclusivity, and accountability.

As you read, I hope you see yourself reflected in these pages. Responsible Tech is not a single discipline or profession. It's a movement; one that thrives when we bring together diverse perspectives to tackle our shared challenges. Your voice, skills, and experiences are essential to shaping technology that works for everyone. Together, we can reimagine and reshape our tech future in our collective direction.

Our Approach to Tackling Thorny Problems

What happens after the general public widely understands and agrees that we have a "tech problem"? While many organizations and activists focus on awareness-building—a crucial stage since people won't solve problems they don't recognize—All Tech Is Human (ATIH) has always focused on the next stage: solution-making.

Our organization acts as a connector, convener, and catalyst for an interdisciplinary, multistakeholder network. By cultivating collaboration, fostering pathways, and centering public interest, ATIH helps society navigate the complex relationship between technology and humanity with ethics, accountability, and human-centeredness at the core. Our approach helps shift the Responsible Tech conversation away from siloed, reactive measures and toward collaborative, adaptive, and holistic solutions that reflect the complexity of real-world tech & society challenges.

Key Parts of Our Approach

Create a Conducive Environment While we have numerous advocacy organizations woven into our network, ATIH is designed to be a sandbox that welcomes the surfacing of values, tensions, tradeoffs, and best practices among all stakeholders.

The Outcome Is Not Predetermined The goal of ATIH is not just to socialize our concepts, but to have continuous points of input from both experts and the general public to collectively understand an issue and work towards mitigation and alignment.

Take a Systems Thinking Approach Tech & society are not separate spheres, but deeply interconnected systems where changes in one area ripple across many others. Instead of treating isolated harms, our approach is on holistic problem-solving that considers the wider social, political, cultural, and economic context.

Bridging the Technical and Humanistic Communities Technical fixes alone will not solve the complex problems we are focused on. Our approach naturally weaves together technologists, designers, ethicists, attorneys, and everyone in between.

Encouraging Community and Collaboration Tackling thorny tech & society issues requires a cohesive ecosystem where we can learn from each other, cross-pollinate our ideas, and collaborate.

An Example of a Thorny Problem

A major topic of recent discussion and media coverage is the rise of so-called "nudify" apps, which have caused disruption in high schools and beyond. These tools use artificial intelligence (AI) or other image-editing techniques to generate fake nude images of people, most often women, from otherwise clothed photos. They fall under the broader category of deepfake or non-consensual intimate image (NCII) tools and are widely condemned for facilitating harassment, exploitation, and abuse.

The issue of nudify apps spans ethics, education, technical interventions, parenting, policy, payment systems, and more. The Responsible Tech workforce addressing these harms sits at the intersection of Trust & Safety (T&S) and Responsible AI (RAI).

This is a classic example of a thorny tech and society challenge that demands a holistic response, weaving together diverse disciplines, stakeholders, and levers of change. It also highlights a persistent tension between privacy protections on the one hand, and harm reduction on the other. Efforts by major platforms to remove non-consensual images have often driven their distribution to more privacy-oriented platforms, such as Telegram, as well as to the dark web.

Another key question is where to focus mitigation efforts. Options include targeting perpetrators who generate or share the images, the creators of the underlying AI models, the platforms that distribute or host the content, or the payment systems that support these services. Some jurisdictions also criminalize possession or viewing of such content – for example, South Korea has pursued this approach. When perpetrators are minors, additional challenges arise in ensuring penalties are proportionate to both the harm caused and the offender's age; the earlier "sexting" debates exposed the need to calibrate legal responses accordingly.

The challenges posed by nudify apps underscore the interplay between civil society, government, industry, and academia. In many cases, research and advocacy by civil society groups have informed and influenced policymakers. In the United States, Congress passed the TAKE IT DOWN Act in May 2025, which criminalizes the publication of non-consensual intimate images. The law increases pressure on platforms to act, which in turn shapes their policies and enforcement.

Every intervention, such as the TAKE IT DOWN Act, can trigger unintended consequences, illustrating why addressing nudify apps requires an iterative, adaptive approach that balances evolving technology, harm reduction, and the protection of civil liberties. ATIH has researched the topic of balancing privacy and child safety in encrypted environments, visit our website to learn more.

What Is Responsible Tech?

Responsible Tech is the interdisciplinary movement and mindset to design, build, and govern technology in ways that align with human values and societal well-being. It ensures that innovation is aligned with the public interest and that technology benefits people while minimizing harm.

The Responsible Tech mindset focuses on avoiding harm, promoting fairness, respecting rights, and being held accountable. It is about shaping technology intentionally, with ethics, humanity, and societal good at the forefront.

Responsible Tech also elevates the role of our Human Agency in shaping our future, as opposed to the future being outside of our control.

Key Principles of Responsible Tech

HUMAN-CENTRIC
Technology should serve people's well-being, not just efficiency or profit.

ETHICAL
Address issues of fairness, bias, privacy, and accountability.

PLURALISTIC
Value a cross-pollination of different backgrounds, disciplines, and perspectives.

TRANSPARENT & EXPLAINABLE
Make systems understandable to users and stakeholders.

SUSTAINABLE
Technology should consider environmental impact and long-term consequences.

ACCOUNTABLE
Organizations and creators must take responsibility for the impacts of their products.

State of Responsible Tech

Responsible Tech has moved from a niche conversation among ethicists and digital rights advocates into mainstream policy, corporate strategy, and media. Responsible Tech is no longer an emerging idea; it's an established but fragmented field wrestling with its identity, impact, and power in shaping the trajectory of technology. Most large tech companies now have ethics teams, governance frameworks, or external advisory boards, and governments worldwide are building regulatory regimes around AI, data, and platforms. However, layoffs in some Responsible Tech-oriented teams (Google, Twitter/X, Microsoft, Meta, among others) highlight tensions between profit and responsibility.

With this maturation of Responsible Tech from the periphery to being institutionalized, there are now more career tracks, certifications, and standards akin to medicine, law, or engineering. One area that has experienced this maturation is the field of Trust & Safety, which now has major conferences like TrustCon and the Trust and Safety Research Conference, academic programs upskilling the next generation, and organizations such as the Integrity Institute and the Trust & Safety Professional Association (TSPA), strengthening community and shared learning. At the same time, it is increasingly under threat as companies cut back moderation budgets and actions are framed in a partisan manner.

Responsible AI Versus AI Safety

There's an undeniable friction between long-term existential risk narratives (AGI alignment, catastrophic risks) and near-term justice-centered work (bias, equity, labor harms). Do we focus on the harms happening today or the theoretical harms that may occur tomorrow? Beneath the surface, a battle over the narrative is being waged to determine which of these two harms should center in our concerns and our strategy of how to best respond to them. While organizations focused on immediate harm are often underfunded, movements like Effective Altruism that are more aligned with "AI Safety" have significant resources and numerous offshoot organizations that influence company culture, build infrastructure to shape discussions on college campuses, and steer the overall media narrative.

Tension Between Profit & Ethics

The tension between profit and ethics plays out in how companies, governments, and civil society approach the design, deployment, and governance of technology. Tech companies operate in hyper-competitive markets where scale, speed, and revenue growth dominate, which can be perceived as being at odds with considerations such as fairness and privacy. The goals of Responsible Tech can be viewed as in conflict with shareholder value maximization, especially when addressing harms means changing currently profitable systems. This occurs when Responsible Tech is seen as a constraint, as opposed to a value proposition. The future of Responsible Tech depends on whether the second model gains enough traction to outweigh short-term profit imperatives.

State of Responsible Tech (continued)

Regulatory Frameworks Differ Across Geographies

Regulatory frameworks and perspectives vary significantly across the globe, reflecting different political systems, cultural values, and economic priorities. Europe has positioned itself as a global leader in tech regulation, most notably with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the EU AI Act, both of which emphasize individual rights, transparency, and accountability. The European approach is risk-based and precautionary, seeking to shape not just domestic practices but also global norms, since companies worldwide often adapt to EU standards. The United States, by contrast, has taken a more fragmented and sector-specific approach. The U.S. relies on agencies like the FTC for enforcement, state-level initiatives such as the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), and voluntary corporate commitments. This reflects a more market-driven philosophy, balancing innovation with selective accountability.

In the Global South, regulatory approaches are diverse and still developing. Countries like India are advancing large-scale data governance laws, while Kenya, Brazil, and others are experimenting with rules around digital IDs, content moderation, and online safety. These regions often face a capacity gap: the harms of technology are significant, but the resources for enforcement, oversight, and technical expertise are limited. Meanwhile, China has built a model of state-driven governance, combining strict data and AI regulations with extensive state surveillance and control, aligning regulation with national security and political priorities rather than civil liberties.

Collectively, this patchwork of approaches highlights a global tension: while technology is borderless, regulation remains highly localized and value-dependent, creating challenges for companies, policymakers, and civil society trying to establish consistent standards for Responsible Tech.

Bridging Technical and Non-Technical Communities

Bridging technical and non-technical communities is at the heart of Responsible Tech, since the most pressing challenges (i.e., algorithmic bias, online safety, and data governance) cannot be solved from a single vantage point. Technologists bring expertise in how systems are built and deployed, while policymakers, ethicists, advocates, and community leaders offer critical insight into social impacts, governance needs, and crucial lived experience. Creating shared spaces for dialogue helps translate complex technical concepts into accessible language for non-specialists, while also ensuring that social values and public concerns are meaningfully incorporated into technical design. Bridging these communities into a multidisciplinary, participatory effort, where diverse voices guide the development of technologies that serve the public good, is challenging but essential.

Responsible Tech Careers

Working in Responsible Tech is important because it ensures that the technologies defining our future are designed with humanity, fairness, and accountability at their core. It's about shifting from "what can we build?" to "what should we build—and how do we build it responsibly?"

Responsible Tech is a chance to connect personal and societal values with professional work. It's a career path that combines technical skills, ethics, and social impact, making it deeply fulfilling and intellectually stimulating. The most crucial skills are a blend of technical and "soft" or humanistic skills.

HUMAN "SOFT" SKILLS

Institutional Navigation: Understanding how decisions really get made

Coalition Building: Creating alliances across competing interests

Responsible Tech Careers (continued)

Important impacts of building a career in Responsible Tech include:

Recognizing that Technology Shapes Everything
Technology has significant impacts—it impacts democracy, economies, education, health, culture, etc. Therefore, the decisions made today affect our tomorrow. Working in Responsible Tech ensures those decisions put a premium on aligning with societal wellbeing.

Creating Safeguards
Without foresight, technology can enable bias, misinformation, surveillance, exploitation, or exclusion. Responsible Tech professionals anticipate risks and build safeguards.

Reducing Harm
Mitigating or minimizing the damage or negative consequences connected with the development, deployment, and use of technology. The reduction of harm is a reactive measure deriving from feedback, research around impact, and the misuse of technology from its intended purposes.

Building Trust
Trust in technology is eroding due to exposure of data misuse, harmful algorithms, and opaque decision-making. Responsible Tech restores trust by embedding transparency, accountability, and fairness. Trust is essential: without it, adoption slows, backlash grows, and innovation stalls.

Driving Inclusive Innovation
Responsible Tech expands who gets a voice in shaping technology—not just engineers or executives, but also ethicists, civil society, policymakers, and impacted communities. This inclusivity leads to better products and more equitable outcomes.

Responding to Regulation & Global Change
Governments worldwide (EU AI Act, U.S. state laws, UN initiatives) are now demanding oversight of AI and digital platforms. Working in Responsible Tech means being at the forefront of shaping standards and compliance frameworks that will define the next decade and beyond.

Responsible Tech Careers: Responsible AI

Responsible AI (RAI) refers to the design, development, deployment, and governance of artificial intelligence systems in a way that aligns with ethical principles, respects human rights, ensures transparency and accountability, and mitigates harm to individuals, communities, and society.

RAI emphasizes that technological progress must be guided by values such as fairness, inclusivity, safety, privacy, and social good, with particular concern given to human-centered, multistakeholder, and participatory processes.

RAI roles exist in large tech companies, AI startups, consulting firms, governmental and multilateral organizations, and research and policy organizations. By embracing RAI practices, companies can build trust with consumers, comply with emerging regulations, and contribute to a sustainable digital future.

RAI practitioners possess a socio-technical skillset; in addition to staying informed on the evolving policy landscape, "soft" skills like critical thinking, ethical reasoning, and multistakeholder collaboration are essential to building an impactful and rewarding career in RAI.

Daily tasks may include assessing built and bought AI systems for risk, conducting rigorous testing to ensure algorithmic fairness, and collaborating with diverse teams to address potential societal impacts.

Job titles for RAI practitioners include:

- Sr. Director, Responsible AI Testing, Evaluation, and Alignment
- Technical AI Ethicist/AI Red Teamer
- Director, Responsible AI
- Research Scientist, Model Safety

Typical Roles in Responsible Tech: RAI

A Director of Responsible AI is a senior professional who oversees an organization's strategy, governance, and implementation of ethical and responsible AI practices. This role is about moving beyond principles into practice: ensuring that AI systems are developed, deployed, and monitored in ways that are fair, transparent, safe, and compliant with emerging regulations. The role typically sits at the intersection of tech strategy, governance, and ethics. This role can be found at major consulting firms, large tech companies, multilateral organizations, and highly regulated industries such as finance or pharmaceuticals. This role provides strategic direction, oversees governance frameworks, and works with cross-functional teams to embed responsible practices into the AI lifecycle.

Key Responsibilities
Strategy & Governance: Define and oversee the organization's Responsible AI framework, policies, and standards.
Risk Oversight: Lead risk assessments for AI models (bias, safety, privacy, explainability, environmental impact).
Compliance: Ensure alignment with legal and regulatory requirements (e.g., EU AI Act, U.S. state-level AI laws, GDPR).
Cross-Functional Collaboration: Partner with product, engineering, legal, and compliance teams to embed Responsible AI practices from design to deployment.
Transparency & Accountability: Oversee documentation practices (model cards, impact assessments, transparency reports).
External Engagement: Represent the organization in industry groups, regulatory consultations, and public forums on AI governance.
Education & Culture: Drive employee training and awareness programs on Responsible AI principles and practices.
Monitoring & Auditing: Establish ongoing evaluation of AI systems and coordinate internal/external audits.

Minimum Qualifications
Bachelor's degree in Computer Science, Data Science, Law, Public Policy, or related field; Master's or PhD preferred.
7+ years of experience in AI, compliance, or governance, with leadership experience.
Strong understanding of AI/ML systems, risks (bias, fairness, explainability, safety), and societal impacts.
Familiarity with AI governance frameworks (NIST AI RMF, OECD Principles, ISO/IEC standards).
Excellent communication and leadership skills, able to influence executives and technical teams.

Typical Roles in Responsible Tech: RAI (continued)

A Technical AI Ethicist or AI Red Teamer is a data scientist who brings technical depth to the assessment of AI products, models, and applications, in order to identify the best technical mitigations to identified risks, testing AI models through an adversarial approach. These professionals help organizations gain a deep understanding of how models and products may be leveraged by malign actors or through unanticipated use to cause harm. In addition to adversarial testing, an AI Red Teamer analyzes current safety trends and develops solutions to detect and mitigate risk, while working cross-functionally with security, engineering, data science, and AI Research teams.

Key Responsibilities
Adversarial Testing: Provide technical leadership in designing, prototyping, and implementing comprehensive adversarial testing strategies, including both automated and manual adversarial testing approaches.
Safety and Robustness: Contribute to the development of detection models and safety guardrails to prevent and mitigate risks posed by bad actors. Research and implement innovative techniques for enhancing AI safety and robustness, drawing from both open-source and internal tools.
Technical Research & Implementation: Write clean, efficient, well-documented code (in Python) to support research efforts and facilitate the evaluation of AI systems.
Testing Execution and Collaboration: Participate in scoping, documenting, and implementing tests with partner teams, including the implementation of mitigations identified during testing. Test for technical vulnerabilities, model vulnerabilities, and harm/abuse including but not limited to bias, toxicity, and inaccuracy.
Reporting, Documentation, and Continuous Learning: Write reports covering the goals and outcomes of testing operations, including significant observations and recommendations.

Minimum Qualifications
A related technical degree required.
5-7 years experience in Software Engineering, AI ethics, AI research, or similar roles.
Experience creating heuristic-based detection logic and rules for identifying anomalous or suspicious activity in systems and networks (e.g. log analysis, user behavior analytics).
Experience using SQL and relational databases. Ability to use Python, R, or other scripting languages to perform data analysis at scale.
Excellent written and oral communication skills, as well as social skills, including the ability to articulate technical concepts to both technical and non-technical audiences.

Responsible Tech Careers: Trust & Safety

Trust & Safety (T&S) refers to the strategies, policies, and practices organizations implement to protect users, employees, and stakeholders from harm while fostering a secure and trustworthy environment. This concept typically applies to online platforms, social media, e-commerce, and technology companies, focusing on minimizing risks such as fraud, abuse, harassment, and harmful content.

In a broader context, 'Trust' refers to the confidence that users or stakeholders have in a platform or organization to act in their best interests, uphold privacy, and maintain security. 'Safety' refers to the measures put in place to protect individuals from threats, both physical and digital, ensuring a safe user experience. Together, Trust & Safety efforts aim to create environments where users feel protected, respected, and confident in their interactions.

The Trust & Safety field is evolving, and difficult tradeoffs and challenges are emerging in the work of promoting a secure and trustworthy digital ecosystem. T&S roles exist in social media platforms, gaming companies, third-party vendors, AI companies, consulting firms, research institutions, and governmental and regulatory bodies.

Typical Roles in Responsible Tech: T&S

A Trust & Safety (T&S) Manager is a professional who leads the teams, policies, and processes that keep digital platforms safe and trustworthy. They balance user safety, platform integrity, and legal/regulatory compliance while protecting freedom of expression and fostering healthy communities. A Trust & Safety Manager is most often hired by digital platforms with user-generated content, online marketplaces, financial/fintech platforms, and regulators—anywhere safety, integrity, and trust are core to the product or service. This includes social media platforms, large tech companies, and gaming companies.

Key Responsibilities

Responsible Tech Career Landscape

The Responsible Tech sector is experiencing significant changes, with the job market being reshaped by technological, political, social, and economic factors. Our 2025 careers report "Navigating the Shifting Sands of the Responsible Tech Job Market", written by ATIH Fellow Deb Donig and based on a survey of our community and broader industry data, provides a snapshot of this evolving landscape.

According to the careers report, Responsible Tech professionals face a complex environment characterized by a "paradox of abundance and scarcity." There are more opportunities than ever before, but they require a unique combination of skills that may not have existed a few years ago. This period is described as "the great recalibration," driven by the rapid advancement of AI, regulatory complexities, and the need for professionals who can effectively translate between technical, policy, and business domains.

The most severely impacted group is entry-level candidates, who are caught in a "double impossibility": they cannot get jobs without experience, nor can they get experience without a job. The aforementioned report highlights that about 75% of jobs labeled "entry-level" require prior experience. This dilemma is a major reason that our organization is heavily focused on providing mentorship and community building, along with working groups and volunteer opportunities. Entry-level candidates increasingly are looking for initial experience to break into the field.

The ATIH survey utilized in our report revealed a significant gap between the skills listed in job descriptions and the skills professionals actually need to succeed. The most crucial skills are a blend of technical and "soft" or humanistic skills. The latter is often the differentiator that determines who gets hired. The highest-value skill emerging from our data is "translation," which involves blending business, ethical, technical, and legal frameworks and communicating effectively across different teams and organizational structures.

Overall, success in the current Responsible Tech job market depends on the ability to navigate ambiguity, translate between conflicting demands, and create value by blending deep competence in one domain (technical, policy, or ethics) with practical experience in the others.

Intersection 1: Responsible AI and Trust & Safety

Responsible AI and Trust & Safety
Trust & Safety (T&S) and Responsible AI (RAI) are distinct but increasingly interconnected fields. T&S traditionally focuses on platform governance and user safety, tackling issues like misinformation, harassment, hate speech, and scams through policy enforcement and content moderation. Responsible AI, on the other hand, emphasizes the ethical design and deployment of AI systems, addressing fairness, accountability, transparency, and bias. As platforms and services increasingly rely on AI models to filter, recommend, and generate content, these two domains overlap: T&S depends on RAI to ensure enforcement tools are fair and explainable, while RAI relies on T&S to ground its principles in real-world harms.

This intersection is most visible in areas like algorithmic recommendation, automated moderation, and generative AI misuse. Together, the two fields face shared challenges of scale, bias, resource pressures, and global variation in norms. Viewed as a continuum, Responsible AI shapes the upstream design of systems, while Trust & Safety manages downstream harms in deployment and use. Their integration is becoming critical as regulators, industry, and civil society increasingly treat ethical AI design and platform safety as inseparable pillars of responsible technology governance.

Why has this intersection occurred?
- While T&S teams have traditionally moderated content, it is increasingly AI models that now decide what to amplify or recommend.
- RAI provides the technical guardrails (e.g., red-teaming, model alignment, safety filters) that feed into T&S enforcement.
- T&S rules are only as fair as the tools enforcing them, which intersect with the RAI teams responsible for the tools.
- Both RAI and T&S rely on automation to handle massive scale, but automation introduces bias and error. The fields are facing the same challenge.
- Some companies are merging or closely aligning their T&S and Responsible AI functions to reduce silos.
- Safety and fairness can't be achieved without tackling both system design (RAI) and platform governance (T&S).
- Both T&S and RAI are part of the same "river." T&S is often focused on downstream harm management, while RAI is focused on upstream harm management.

Intersection 2: Responsible AI and Public Interest Technology

Responsible AI and Public Interest Technology
Responsible AI (RAI) and Public Interest Technology (PIT) share a common goal of ensuring that technology benefits society, but they approach it from different starting points. RAI focuses on the design and governance of AI systems, emphasizing fairness, accountability, transparency, privacy, and bias mitigation. It often works at the technical and policy levels, developing tools and frameworks to ensure AI systems are built responsibly. PIT, by contrast, takes a broader view of technology's role in society, encompassing areas like digital government, civic tech, broadband access, and cybersecurity, with an emphasis on serving the public good and aligning technology with democratic values.

The intersection between the two is strongest where AI impacts public life — such as welfare allocation, healthcare, education, and policing. Here, RAI provides the ethical and technical safeguards, while PIT supplies the civic and institutional structures to apply them. Both fields stress inclusion and equity, highlighting how marginalized communities are disproportionately affected by technological harms. Together, they represent complementary efforts: Responsible AI focuses on how to build AI systems responsibly, while Public Interest Technology ensures those systems—and technology more broadly—are deployed in ways that genuinely advance the public interest.

Why has this intersection occurred?
- Both emphasize inclusion and harm reduction, particularly for marginalized communities who are often disproportionately impacted by algorithmic systems.
- Many professionals in RAI move into the public sector, non-profits, or academia under the PIT umbrella, bringing technical skills into civic spaces.
- Universities, think tanks, and philanthropy invest in both PIT and RAI as part of a broader movement for Responsible Tech.
- Both fields aim to ensure technology serves people, not just profit — RAI at the system design level, PIT at the societal and governance level.

While Responsible AI focuses on how we build AI systems responsibly and Public Interest Technology focuses on how technology serves democratic, civic, and societal goals, the increasing concern to ensure that AI systems are aligned with democratic and societal goals intertwines the fields.

How does ATIH define a Responsible Tech organization?
Generally speaking, we consider a Responsible Tech organization one that:
- Aligns with the broader goal of technology working in the public interest.
- Engages across disciplines for a full perspective of tech & society issues.
- Produces resources that are freely available to the community.
- Centers human impact in the design, development, and deployment of technologies.
- Values public input and feedback in their process.

Tackling Hard Problems Depends on a Strong Ecosystem

Thorny tech & society issues are not problems with simple solutions; they're systemic challenges with technical, legal, cultural, and economic roots. No single discipline (whether that's computer science, law, policy, or design) can see the whole picture, much less solve it alone. Responsible Tech, as a concept, is a shift from siloed thinking towards one based on interdisciplinary appreciation, cross-pollination, and collaboration.

A strong Responsible Tech ecosystem creates the necessary environment for solution-making; one where diverse expertise and lived experience meet, translate, and iterate. A vibrant and cohesive ecosystem leads to a common language and standards, clear career pathways that allow for broader talent to come in, and communities of practice that shorten the distance between a problem surfaced and a solution tested. When researchers, product teams, civil society, and policymakers can move from siloed discussions to collaboration, we also move from reactive to proactive.

Incentives also change in an ecosystem. Companies gain peers and pressure to internalize safety and fairness as markers of quality, not just compliance. Policymakers gain timely, technical input before rules calcify. Journalists and advocates gain credible, up-to-date context to inform the public. Most importantly, impacted communities gain channels to shape how technologies are imagined, built, and deployed—not after the fact, but upstream where choices are made.

If we want real progress, we have to invest not only in better models or laws, but in the connective tissue between them. Convenings that welcome disagreement, repositories that make the good work being done easy to adopt, and networks that move quickly across borders and sectors. That's how Responsible Tech becomes less about principles and more about practiced habits and best practices. This, in turn, takes us from a reactive state to one where we can co-create towards a better tech future.

All Tech Is Human Overview

Unwavering Commitment

All Tech Is Human convenes a global community of over 50,000 people committed to tackling thorny tech and society issues through collective understanding, involvement, and action. Founded in 2018, we provide the relational infrastructure for the Responsible Tech ecosystem, creating a conducive environment for solution-making, and rapidly distributing key concepts, and leveraging best practices across our extensive network.

We specialize in initiatives that naturally weave together multiple stakeholders, disciplines, and perspectives to surface values, tensions, trade-offs, and best practices for problem-solving. Together, we make and mobilize change toward co-creating a better tech future for all.

Our Next Chapter Begins

The Story of All Tech Is Human outlines the need to more participatory, less top-down, more agile, and capable of distributing collective intelligence and insight across a multistakeholder network while moving quickly. This progress happens in two stages:

Stage 1: Shift the narrative and build a cohesive multistakeholder, multidisciplinary community that can understand important values, tensions, tradeoffs, and best practices.

Stage 2: Utilize the power of a global network to inform and influence key levers of change. Have a tech future that is better aligned with the public interest by having a structure that offers crucial insight and involvement. All Tech Is Human will evolve into a necessary component to speed up the flow of information between stakeholders across civil society, government, industry, and academia.

The shift from stage 1 to stage 2 shows how All Tech Is Human is evolving from being a civil society organization to more of a complex adaptive system that is designed to understand community sentiment, provide access to an extensive interdisciplinary network, and mobilize activity.

Now in stage 2, we remain committed to bringing a range of new activities to those working all levels in Responsible Tech – from ones breaking into the industry to ones defining and running organizations.
