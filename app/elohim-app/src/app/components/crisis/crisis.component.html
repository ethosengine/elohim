What Grandma Is Trading

When Grandma signs up for a free photo service, she isn't making a one-time choice. She's entering a continuous, micro-incremented auction — her attention, her agency, and the texture of her humanity, auctioned in continuous fractions to whichever ad market clears highest this microsecond. The convenience is real. So is the cost. The architecture forces them into a single bundle.

"The real problem of humanity is the following: We have Paleolithic emotions, medieval institutions and godlike technology."
— Edward O. Wilson

The old internet shipped its three load-bearing concerns — information, value, and governance — separately, and platforms sat in the propagation path of all three. Each leg of that three-legged stool was capturable on its own; and once any one leg was captured, the other two bent toward whoever owned it. The medium pulled value toward the platform; the value pulled governance toward the platform — until the stool was no longer holding anyone up.

Money carries no values

A dollar spent on weapons is identical to a dollar spent on medicine. In light of modern computation, our currencies are astonishingly primitive.

Care generates nothing

A grandmother's weekly hours of childcare, a teacher's dedication, a neighbor's steady presence — real value the ledger cannot see, let alone reward.

Wealth concentrates

Exponential returns to capital, linear returns to labor. The architecture concentrates rather than circulates, by default.

Votes are binary and years apart

Complex preferences compressed into yes/no choices every few years, while the feedback loops in between stay broken enough to ignore.

None of this is fixed from inside the trap. It is not solved by exit — the flip phone costs Grandma the connective tissue of late-life participation, and that loss lands on her family and her neighbors. It is not solved by encryption alone , which protects packets, not relationships. And it is not solved by self-sovereign identity , because sovereignty assumes a kind of solitary competence that nobody — least of all an 84-year-old recovering from a TIA — actually possesses or wants. The substrate itself has to change.
